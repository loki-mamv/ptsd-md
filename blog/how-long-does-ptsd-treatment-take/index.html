How Long Does PTSD Treatment Take?

August 27, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Most first-line PTSD therapy takes about 3 months. Prolonged Exposure runs 8 to 15 weekly sessions, Cognitive Processing Therapy runs 12, and EMDR takes about 3 months of weekly visits, per the VA National Center for PTSD. Medication takes 4 to 6 weeks to start working and is judged at 12 weeks. A massed format can compress the same therapy into 2 weeks.

How many sessions does each PTSD therapy take?

The three therapies below are the ones the 2023 VA/DoD guideline recommends first. The APA 2017 guideline strongly recommends PE, CPT, cognitive therapy and CBT, and conditionally recommends EMDR. Session counts come from the VA National Center for PTSD.

  • Prolonged Exposure (PE): 8 to 15 weekly sessions of 60 to 90 minutes. Treatment lasts about 3 months.
  • Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT): 12 weekly sessions of 60 minutes for individuals, 90 minutes for groups. About 3 months.
  • EMDR: 50 to 90 minute sessions, about 3 months of weekly visits. Many clinics plan 6 to 12 sessions.
  • Written Exposure Therapy (WET): a brief 5-session option.

Symptoms usually start to drop before the course ends. The VA notes that people in PE "may start to feel better after a few sessions," and says the same about EMDR. Those early gains are a good sign, but they are not the finish line. The full course exists because each session builds on the last one.

You can compare these options side by side in our guide to PTSD treatment options. If you are leaning toward EMDR, read what to expect in EMDR first.

Can PTSD treatment be finished in two weeks?

Yes, for PE. A 2018 trial in JAMA randomized 370 active-duty service members with PTSD. One group got 10 PE sessions in 2 weeks. Another got the same 10 sessions over 8 weeks. Massed PE was noninferior to spaced PE at both the 2-week and 12-week follow-ups.

The loss-of-diagnosis numbers were also similar. In the massed group, 45.4% no longer met PTSD criteria at the 2-week follow-up. In the spaced group, 46.4% no longer met criteria after treatment. Massed formats now run in several VA and academic programs.

The trade-off is intensity. Daily sessions mean daily homework and daily exposure to trauma memories. Some people prefer that. They finish before avoidance has time to pull them out of treatment. Others need a week between sessions to absorb the work. Both schedules deliver the same total dose.

How long do PTSD medications take to work?

The VA/DoD guideline recommends sertraline, paroxetine or venlafaxine when therapy is not an option or not preferred. The VA says it "may take a few weeks before you notice the effects," and that most people start to feel better in about 4 to 6 weeks.

The FDA labels set the trial window at 12 weeks. The sertraline label describes two 12-week flexible-dose studies at 50 to 200 mg per day. Completers averaged 146 and 151 mg per day. The paroxetine label cites 12-week trials at 20 to 50 mg once daily. So 12 weeks at a working dose is a fair test.

Medication does not have a natural endpoint the way a 12-session protocol does. The sertraline label reports a maintenance study: 24 weeks of open treatment, then up to 28 more weeks watching for relapse. The VA states plainly that you "will need to keep taking the medication to keep getting the benefits." Some people stop later after therapy or life changes, but that is a decision to make with a prescriber.

Effect sizes also favor therapy. The Watts 2013 meta-analysis of 112 studies found effect sizes of 1.63 for cognitive therapy, 1.08 for exposure and 1.01 for EMDR. Paroxetine scored 0.74, venlafaxine 0.48 and sertraline 0.41. That gap is why the 2023 VA/DoD guideline recommends psychotherapy over pharmacotherapy, citing larger and more persistent improvement.

Does complex PTSD take longer to treat?

Often, yes, but not by as much as many people expect. The VA says the same trauma-focused therapies work for complex PTSD and "generally take 3-4 months." That is one month longer than the standard course. The VA adds that other concerns, such as depression or relationship health, may still need work after the PTSD symptoms improve.

The research supports that view. A 2019 meta-analysis of 51 trials found that CBT and exposure produced moderate to large gains in negative self-concept and relationship problems, the core features of complex PTSD. It also found that childhood-onset trauma predicted poorer outcomes, and that few trials measured emotion regulation at all.

In practice, that means a plan with more than one phase. Trauma-focused work still comes first. Skills for emotion regulation, sleep, and mood may follow. If depression is part of the picture, depression.md covers treatment timelines for that separately. Our comparison of complex PTSD versus PTSD explains how the two diagnoses differ.

What does "done" look like?

Researchers use "loss of diagnosis" as the yardstick. It means you no longer meet the criteria for PTSD on a structured interview. A 2025 meta-analysis in JAMA Psychiatry pooled 34 randomized trials with 3,208 participants and reported these post-treatment rates:

  • Civilians: 56% after PE, 57% after CPT, and 86% after EMDR.
  • Military and veterans: 50% after PE and 44% after CPT.

Two cautions. First, loss of diagnosis does not mean zero symptoms. The VA describes it well: "For many people, treatment can get rid of symptoms altogether. Others find they have fewer symptoms or feel that their symptoms are less intense." Second, the EMDR figure comes from fewer trials with lower-quality evidence, so read it as promising rather than settled.

A practical definition of done is simpler. You can think about the trauma without being hijacked by it. You have stopped avoiding the places, people and reminders that used to control your week. You sleep. Your therapist will often measure this with a PCL-5 score every few sessions, so you can watch the number fall.

Why do people quit early, and how do you avoid it?

Dropout is the main reason treatment takes longer than 3 months. A 2020 meta-analysis found that 16% of people in PTSD therapy trials dropped out. Trauma-focused therapies had higher dropout than other approaches. Real-world clinics usually see higher rates than trials. In the massed PE trial above, only 59% of participants completed the full study.

People leave for predictable reasons. The early sessions raise distress before it falls. Homework takes real time. Life events interrupt a weekly schedule. Avoidance, itself a PTSD symptom, makes skipping a session feel like relief.

A few things help you finish:

  1. Ask about the plan before session one. Know the session count, the homework load, and what week the hardest part usually falls.
  2. Consider a massed format. Two weeks leaves less room for life to interrupt.
  3. Use video sessions. The VA/DoD guideline recommends secure video for validated therapies. It removes the commute as an excuse.
  4. Treat a rough session as expected, not as a sign to stop. Tell your therapist. The pace can be adjusted without ending the protocol.
  5. Track your scores. Watching a PCL-5 number fall is the strongest reason to keep going.

The bottom line

Plan on about 3 months for a standard course of PE, CPT or EMDR, or 2 weeks for massed PE. Expect medication to need 4 to 6 weeks to start working and 12 weeks for a fair trial, with no built-in end date. Complex PTSD adds roughly a month, and often a second phase of work on mood, sleep, and relationships. About half of veterans and more than half of civilians no longer meet PTSD criteria after one full course. The biggest threat to that timeline is stopping early, so pick the format you can finish.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 12 weeks of therapy enough for PTSD?

For most people, yes. The VA National Center for PTSD says most trauma-focused therapies last about 3 months. In trials, roughly half of veterans and more than half of civilians no longer met PTSD criteria after a standard course. If symptoms remain at the end, you and your therapist can plan more sessions.

Will I feel worse before I feel better in PTSD therapy?

Some people feel more stirred up in the first few sessions when they begin to face trauma memories. The VA notes that many people start to feel better after a few sessions. Tell your therapist if distress spikes so the pace can be adjusted.

How long should I stay on sertraline or paroxetine for PTSD?

The FDA labels for both drugs are based on 12-week trials. A sertraline maintenance study followed people for 24 weeks of open treatment plus up to 28 more weeks. The VA says benefits continue only while you keep taking the medication, so the stop date is a decision to make with your prescriber.

Can I do PTSD therapy over video instead of in person?

Yes. The 2023 VA/DoD guideline recommends secure video teleconferencing for therapies that have been validated in that format, or when in-person care is not available. The session count and timeline are the same as in-person care.

What happens if I finish treatment and PTSD symptoms come back?

A return of symptoms after a stressful event is common and does not mean treatment failed. Booster sessions that revisit the skills you learned often help. The VA advises talking with your therapist about ways to manage remaining symptoms using those skills.
