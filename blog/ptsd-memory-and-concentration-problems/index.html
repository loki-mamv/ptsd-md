Can PTSD Cause Memory And Concentration Problems?

September 9, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Yes. Memory and concentration problems are written into the PTSD diagnosis itself, not tacked on as side effects. The DSM-5-TR lists "inability to recall an important aspect of the traumatic event(s)" and "problems with concentration" as criteria. Neuropsychological testing also finds measurable deficits in verbal learning, processing speed, and attention.

What does the DSM-5 say about memory and concentration in PTSD?

Two separate criteria cover thinking. The VA/DoD Clinical Practice Guideline for Management of Posttraumatic Stress Disorder and Acute Stress Disorder (June 2023) reprints the DSM-5-TR criteria in full.

  • Criterion D1, under negative alterations in cognitions and mood: "Inability to recall an important aspect of the traumatic event(s) (typically due to dissociative amnesia and not to other factors such as head injury, alcohol, or drugs)."
  • Criterion E5, under alterations in arousal and reactivity: "Problems with concentration."

Criterion D requires two of its seven items. Criterion E requires two of its six. So a person can meet the full diagnosis without either cognitive item. Many people report both anyway.

The VA National Center for PTSD notes that DSM-5-TR, published in March 2022, made no changes to the adult PTSD criteria. NIMH lists "having trouble concentrating and sleeping" among common reactions after a traumatic event. For the wider symptom picture, see our overview of PTSD symptoms.

How is a trauma memory gap different from everyday forgetfulness?

The DSM criterion is narrow. It describes a gap in recall for part of the traumatic event, attributed to dissociative amnesia. It explicitly excludes gaps caused by head injury, alcohol, or drugs. Someone may remember the drive to the scene and the hospital, but not the twenty minutes in between.

That is different from the daily fog many people with PTSD describe: losing keys, rereading the same paragraph, forgetting why you walked into a room. Those complaints belong to Criterion E5, concentration, not to Criterion D1. They are ongoing and general. A trauma gap is fixed in time and tied to one event.

The distinction matters. A trauma memory gap does not mean the memory sits intact somewhere waiting to be retrieved accurately. It also does not rule out PTSD when the memory is complete. We cover that question in PTSD without remembering the trauma.

What does cognitive testing actually show in PTSD?

The largest synthesis is a meta-analysis by Scott and colleagues, published in Psychological Bulletin in 2015. It pooled 60 studies with 4,108 participants: 1,779 with PTSD, 1,446 trauma-exposed comparisons, and 895 comparisons without trauma exposure.

All nine cognitive domains showed a significant deficit. Effect sizes ran from d = -0.29 to d = -0.62, largest first:

  • Verbal learning: d = -0.62
  • Speed of information processing: d = -0.59
  • Attention and working memory: d = -0.50
  • Verbal memory: d = -0.46
  • Executive functions: d = -0.45
  • Language: d = -0.43
  • Visuospatial ability: d = -0.38
  • Visual learning: d = -0.32
  • Visual memory: d = -0.29

By the usual convention, d values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 are small, medium, and large. The pattern is a medium-sized problem with learning new verbal material, working at speed, and holding attention. Visual memory is affected least. These are group averages, not a forecast for any individual. Deficits were larger in treatment-seeking samples (d = -0.65) than in community samples (d = -0.30).

Why does PTSD affect thinking: sleep, arousal, or head injury?

Several factors stack up. Sleep disturbance is its own PTSD criterion, and poor sleep degrades attention and memory by itself. A 2025 Journal of Sleep Research study of US military veterans found 4.0% screened positive for both PTSD and insomnia. Against PTSD alone, that comorbid group scored far lower on a cognitive functioning scale (d = 1.05). If broken sleep is your main driver, insomnia.md covers that side.

Hypervigilance taxes attention directly. Scanning a room for threat draws on the same limited attention a work task needs. Our piece on hypervigilance in PTSD explains that loop.

Depression and substance use travel with PTSD. Even so, the meta-analysis found no significant effect from the proportion of participants with major depression. Rates of alcohol or drug use disorders did not move the effect sizes either. Head injury did not explain the findings either. Studies that excluded even mild head trauma still found d = -0.50.

In veterans, PTSD and traumatic brain injury frequently coexist. The VA National Center for PTSD notes that both can involve sleep problems, irritability, and memory problems. It advises using injury history, symptom timeline, and neuropsychological testing to separate them.

Why is PTSD often mistaken for ADHD?

The surface symptoms line up. Distractibility, restlessness, irritability, weak working memory, and unfinished tasks appear in both. Adult ADHD screening questionnaires ask about exactly those things.

The meta-analysis offers an unusual clue about how much overlap exists. Studies that screened out participants with ADHD found a smaller PTSD deficit (d = -0.27). Studies that did not screen them out found d = -0.51 (p = .009). Some of the attention deficit attributed to PTSD in the literature is carried by co-occurring ADHD.

The two also genuinely co-occur. A controlled family study in the Journal of Clinical Psychiatry found lifetime PTSD in 10.0% of adults with ADHD versus 1.6% of controls. Timeline is the practical discriminator. ADHD symptoms are present from childhood. PTSD concentration problems begin or worsen after the traumatic event.

Does treatment improve cognition, and when should you get tested?

Treatment helps, and the deficits look at least partly reversible. A 2018 study in the British Journal of Clinical Psychology measured cognition before and after trauma-focused psychotherapy. It found small to medium improvements in verbal memory, information processing speed, and executive functioning (Cohen's d 0.16 to 0.68). Bigger drops in PTSD symptoms went with better post-treatment cognitive scores.

Cognition also predicts treatment response in the other direction. A study protocol reviewing this literature cites trials where baseline verbal learning predicted who responded to trauma-focused therapy. In one 140-person sample, immediate verbal memory predicted remission over 16 weeks of treatment.

Ask a clinician about a neuropsychological evaluation in these situations:

  1. The cognitive complaints do not rise and fall with your other PTSD symptoms.
  2. There is a head injury, blast exposure, or concussion history.
  3. Work or school is failing even though PTSD treatment is going well.
  4. An ADHD, learning disorder, or early dementia question needs a real answer.

A full battery measures the same domains listed above. It also helps separate effort, mood, and sleep effects from a stable deficit. Bring your medication list plus a written trauma and injury timeline.

The bottom line

PTSD can cause two different memory problems, and they are not interchangeable. A gap in recall for part of the trauma is Criterion D1. Ongoing trouble concentrating is Criterion E5. On formal testing, the most reliable deficits are in verbal learning, processing speed, and attention, medium in size on average across 60 studies. Sleep loss, hypervigilance, depression, dissociation, and in veterans a head injury history all add to the load. None of that means the damage is permanent. Cognition improved over trauma-focused psychotherapy in the measured studies, and a neuropsychological evaluation can tell you what is actually driving the fog.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is PTSD brain fog the same as dissociation?

No, though they can occur together. Dissociation is a felt sense of detachment from yourself or your surroundings, and time can seem to skip. Brain fog is slower processing and weaker attention that you notice while fully present. Both can make it hard to lay down new memories.

Can PTSD memory problems look like early dementia?

They can worry people that way, especially in older adults. The profile differs, though. PTSD deficits cluster in verbal learning, processing speed, and attention, and they fluctuate with sleep, mood, and stress. Progressive, steadily worsening decline needs its own medical workup rather than a PTSD explanation.

Will treating my sleep fix my concentration?

It often helps a lot. Sleep disturbance is a PTSD criterion in its own right, and veterans with both PTSD and insomnia scored much worse on cognitive functioning than those with PTSD alone. Treating insomnia will not always resolve everything, since hyperarousal and depression also contribute.

Do PTSD medications cause memory problems?

Some medicines used around PTSD, including benzodiazepines and certain sleep agents, can dull attention and memory. Never stop a prescribed medicine on your own. Bring a full medication list to your clinician and ask whether any of it could be contributing to the cognitive complaints.

What happens during a neuropsychological evaluation?

You complete several hours of standardized tests covering learning, memory, attention, processing speed, language, and executive function. The psychologist compares your scores against age and education norms and reviews your history. The report describes your cognitive profile and offers practical accommodations for work or school.
