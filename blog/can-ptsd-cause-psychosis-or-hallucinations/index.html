Can PTSD Cause Psychosis or Hallucinations?

Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: PTSD can cause experiences that look like psychosis, and sometimes it causes true psychotic symptoms. Flashbacks and dissociation are part of PTSD itself, and most people who have them still know what is real. A smaller group has hallucinations or delusions that go past typical PTSD, a pattern researchers call PTSD with secondary psychotic features. Trauma, mainly in childhood, also raises the risk of a later psychotic disorder.

Are PTSD flashbacks the same as hallucinations?

No, but they can feel close. The DSM-5 criteria summarized by the VA National Center for PTSD list flashbacks as a re-experiencing symptom, next to unwanted memories and nightmares. In a flashback, the body and mind act as if the trauma is happening again. A person may see, hear, or smell parts of the event.

The key difference is content and context. Flashback images come from the trauma and are set off by reminders. They pass, and afterward the person usually knows they were memories. The National Institute of Mental Health defines psychosis as symptoms where "there has been some loss of contact with reality." Hallucinations in psychosis often have no link to one event, and the person may fully believe them.

What is the dissociative subtype of PTSD?

DSM-5 added a dissociative subtype for people who meet full PTSD criteria and also have ongoing depersonalization or derealization. The VA summary of the criteria defines them this way:

  • Depersonalization: feeling like an outside observer of yourself, or as if "this is not happening to me."
  • Derealization: a sense that the world is unreal, distant, or distorted.

These states can be scary, and people sometimes fear they are "losing their mind." But dissociation is not psychosis. The person can usually describe the feeling as strange or unreal, which shows that reality testing is still intact.

The subtype is not rare. In a nationally representative study of 1,484 U.S. veterans (Tsai and colleagues, 2015), 19.2% of those with lifetime PTSD and 16.1% with past-month PTSD screened positive for it. Veterans with the subtype had more severe PTSD, more depression, and more alcohol problems.

Can PTSD cause true psychotic symptoms?

Yes, in some people. Researchers use the term PTSD with secondary psychotic features (PTSD-SP) for psychotic symptoms that occur with PTSD but without a primary psychotic disorder such as schizophrenia. A 2019 review by Compean and Hamner reports emerging evidence that PTSD-SP may be a distinct form of PTSD, with genetic and brain differences from both non-psychotic PTSD and primary psychotic disorders.

Much of this work comes from veterans. A 1999 study of Vietnam combat veterans states that psychotic symptoms may occur in up to 40% of patients with combat-related PTSD. In that study, 22 of 45 veterans without a primary psychotic disorder had psychotic features. Those with more severe psychotic symptoms also had more severe PTSD, and 68% of them had major depression.

One finding matters for the flashback question. In the same study, re-experiencing scores did not track positive psychotic symptoms. That suggests the psychotic features were not just very intense flashbacks.

Common features reported in PTSD-SP include voices, often critical or linked to the trauma, and paranoid beliefs. Strong hypervigilance can shade into suspicion that others mean harm. When that suspicion becomes a fixed belief that does not change with evidence, clinicians consider it a delusion.

Does trauma raise the risk of schizophrenia or other psychotic disorders?

The evidence says yes, as a risk factor rather than a sole cause. A 2012 meta-analysis in Schizophrenia Bulletin (Varese and colleagues) pooled 36 studies of childhood adversity, including sexual, physical, and emotional abuse, neglect, parental death, and bullying. People with these experiences had about 2.8 times the odds of psychosis (odds ratio 2.78).

The link held across study types, including prospective cohorts that tracked people forward in time. The authors estimated a population attributable risk of 33%. That figure is a statistical estimate of how much psychosis might be linked to childhood adversity if the link were causal. It does not mean trauma alone causes a third of cases.

So the relationship runs both ways. PTSD can bring psychotic features, and people with psychotic disorders often carry trauma histories and untreated PTSD.

How do clinicians tell PTSD from a psychotic disorder?

The Compean and Hamner review notes that overlapping symptoms make the diagnosis hard. A careful assessment usually looks at several things:

  1. Link to the trauma. Are the voices or images tied to the event, or are they unrelated?
  2. Insight. Does the person recognize the experience as a memory or a trick of the mind, or fully believe it?
  3. Timing. Did the symptoms start after the trauma, and do they flare with reminders?
  4. Other signs. NIMH lists early signs of psychosis such as confused speech, trouble thinking clearly, social withdrawal, poor self-care, and unusual intense ideas. Disorganized thinking points more toward a primary psychotic disorder.
  5. Other causes. Clinicians rule out substance use, medication effects, severe sleep loss, and medical conditions.

Depression matters too. Severe depression can bring its own psychotic features, and it often travels with PTSD-SP. A structured interview by a psychiatrist or psychologist is the most reliable way to sort this out.

What treatment helps when PTSD and psychosis overlap?

Trauma-focused therapy still works, and it appears safe. For years, psychosis kept people out of PTSD trials. A 2015 randomized trial in JAMA Psychiatry (van den Berg and colleagues) tested prolonged exposure and EMDR in 155 people with a psychotic disorder and chronic PTSD.

  • After 8 weekly sessions, 56.6% in the prolonged exposure group and 60.0% in the EMDR group no longer met PTSD criteria, compared with 27.7% on the waiting list.
  • Severe adverse events were not more common with therapy (2 with prolonged exposure, 1 with EMDR, 4 on the waiting list).
  • Gains held at 6-month follow-up.

The authors concluded that excluding people with psychosis from these treatments "may not be justifiable." For how these therapies differ, see prolonged exposure vs cognitive processing therapy.

Medication evidence is thinner. The 2019 review found that risperidone and quetiapine were the only second-generation antipsychotics studied in randomized trials for PTSD-SP. The authors said the evidence is not enough to recommend them as standard treatment. A psychiatrist may still use medication case by case, based on symptoms and safety.

Many people with PTSD also live with depression, anxiety, or other conditions. Mental.md covers these related conditions.

When should you get urgent help?

Get help the same day if you or someone close to you:

  • hears voices that give commands, mainly to harm self or others;
  • holds fixed beliefs that others are plotting harm, and acts on them;
  • seems unable to tell what is real for more than a short time;
  • has thoughts of suicide.

In the U.S., call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. Veterans can call 988 and press 1 for the Veterans Crisis Line. If there is immediate danger, call 911.

The bottom line

Flashbacks and dissociation are core parts of PTSD, not signs of psychosis on their own. Some people with PTSD do develop true hallucinations or delusions, and childhood trauma raises the odds of a later psychotic disorder. A careful assessment can tell these apart. Trauma-focused therapy helps even when psychosis is present, so new or worsening symptoms deserve prompt care.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hearing voices a normal part of PTSD?

Hearing voices is not one of the core DSM-5 symptoms of PTSD. Some people with PTSD do hear voices, often critical or linked to the trauma. It should always be checked by a mental health professional, since it can signal PTSD with psychotic features, severe depression, or a separate psychotic disorder.

Can dissociation turn into psychosis?

Dissociation and psychosis are different processes. In depersonalization or derealization, people usually know the feeling is strange, while psychosis involves a loss of contact with reality. Both can occur in the same person, so a clinician should assess any new or worsening symptoms.

Do antipsychotics treat PTSD?

Antipsychotics are not a standard first-line PTSD treatment. A 2019 review found that only risperidone and quetiapine had been tested in randomized trials for PTSD with psychotic features, and the evidence was not strong enough to recommend them routinely. A psychiatrist may still use them in specific cases.

Is trauma therapy safe if I have a psychotic disorder?

A 2015 randomized trial in 155 people with psychotic disorders and PTSD found prolonged exposure and EMDR reduced PTSD without more severe adverse events than a waiting list. The authors concluded that excluding people with psychosis from these therapies may not be justified. Therapy should still be delivered by a trained clinician who knows your full history.

Can a panic attack or extreme stress cause brief hallucinations?

Extreme stress, severe sleep loss, and substance use can all bring on brief perceptual changes. A single short episode is different from ongoing psychosis. If it happens more than once or you are unsure what is real, see a clinician promptly.
