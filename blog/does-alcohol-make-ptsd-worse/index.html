Does Drinking Alcohol Make PTSD Symptoms Worse?

September 24, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Yes. A drink may take the edge off for an hour, but over days and weeks alcohol tends to make PTSD worse. It breaks up sleep, raises next-day anxiety as it wears off, and gets in the way of the emotional processing that recovery needs. The good news: you do not have to quit first to start PTSD treatment, and therapies that treat both problems together work.

Why do so many people with PTSD drink?

Alcohol is a fast, legal way to turn down fear, tension, and memories for a while. The National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA) notes that alcohol is commonly used to numb memories of a traumatic event or to cope with symptoms of posttraumatic stress. That short-term relief is real, which is why the habit is hard to break.

The overlap is large. The VA National Center for PTSD reports that about 45% of adults with PTSD also have problems with drug or alcohol use. Veterans with a history of PTSD are 2 times more likely to have alcohol problems than veterans without it. Looking from the other direction, NIAAA estimates that 15 to 30% of people with alcohol use disorder have PTSD, rising to 50 to 60% among military personnel and veterans.

How does alcohol make PTSD symptoms worse?

The problem is what happens after the first hour. Several effects stack on top of each other:

  • Worse sleep. Many people drink to fall asleep, but the VA points out that alcohol can make sleep less restful. NIAAA adds that even moderate doses alter sleep physiology, and full recovery of sleep can take 30 or more days without alcohol. Poor sleep feeds the irritability and poor focus that PTSD already causes. For more on this, see our guide on whether PTSD nightmares go away.
  • Rebound arousal. As alcohol wears off, the nervous system swings the other way. NIAAA notes that the autonomic hyperactivity of alcohol withdrawal can look like PTSD hyperarousal. Racing heart, jumpiness, and sweating the next morning can feel like PTSD getting worse, and in practice they add to it.
  • Blocked coping. NIAAA describes alcohol as undermining coping by disrupting arousal, sleep, and thinking. Numbing a memory is a form of avoidance, and avoidance is one of the main things that keeps PTSD going.
  • More anger and risk. Drinking lowers the brakes on anger that is already close to the surface. It also raises the chance of new trauma, such as violence, assault, or accidents, according to NIAAA. Our article on PTSD and anger covers that link.

Does a drink or two count, or only heavy drinking?

Most of the research focuses on problem drinking and alcohol use disorder, so the clearest harm shows up with heavy or frequent use. There is no study that sets a "safe" number of drinks for someone with PTSD. What the evidence does show is that even moderate amounts change sleep, according to NIAAA, and sleep is one of the symptoms PTSD hits hardest.

A useful test is why you drink rather than how much. Drinking to sleep, to stop a memory, or to get through a crowded place is drinking to cope. That pattern tends to grow, because the brain learns that alcohol is the fix. If you notice that pattern, it is worth raising with a clinician even if the amount seems small.

Do I have to stop drinking before PTSD treatment?

No. For years many programs made people get sober before they could start trauma therapy. Current guidance has moved away from that. The 2023 VA/DoD PTSD clinical practice guideline includes recommendations for PTSD with co-occurring conditions. The VA summary of that guidance says having one disorder should not stop someone from getting evidence-based treatment for the other.

The same VA summary reports that integrated, trauma-focused treatments that address both disorders show the greatest benefit. People who received trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy along with substance use treatment were more likely to reduce both PTSD symptoms and alcohol use than those who got substance use treatment alone. The first-line trauma therapies are prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, and EMDR. Our comparison of prolonged exposure vs. cognitive processing therapy explains how they differ.

What does integrated treatment look like, and does it work?

The best-studied integrated therapy is COPE (Concurrent Treatment of PTSD and Substance Use Disorders Using Prolonged Exposure). It pairs prolonged exposure for PTSD with relapse-prevention skills for drinking or drug use, in the same sessions with the same therapist.

  • Australia, 2012. A randomized trial by Mills and colleagues in JAMA enrolled 103 people with PTSD and substance dependence. COPE was 13 sessions of 90 minutes each. Adding COPE to usual care produced a significantly larger drop in PTSD severity at 9 months. Substance use improved in both groups by a similar amount, which answered a key worry: trauma therapy did not trigger relapse.
  • U.S. veterans, 2019. Back and colleagues, writing in Addictive Behaviors, randomized 81 veterans with PTSD and a substance use disorder to 12 sessions of COPE or relapse prevention alone. COPE led to much larger drops in PTSD symptoms and higher rates of PTSD remission. Both groups cut substance use. At 6 months, the COPE group had fewer drinks per drinking day.
  • Primary care, 2024. A pilot trial in three VA primary care clinics tested a five-session mix of motivational interviewing and brief prolonged exposure in 63 veterans with PTSD and harmful drinking. Compared with usual care, it produced steeper drops in PTSD severity and heavy drinking.

Medication can help with the drinking side too. The VA summary lists naltrexone, topiramate, disulfiram, N-acetylcysteine, and varenicline as drugs that improve alcohol use in people with PTSD. The VA/DoD guideline recommends against benzodiazepines for PTSD.

When should I get help right away?

Alcohol and PTSD together raise the stakes. Get help soon if you drink more than you plan to, need a drink to sleep most nights, or feel shaky and anxious when you stop. If you drink heavily every day, talk to a clinician before you stop, because withdrawal needs medical guidance.

If you are thinking about hurting yourself, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. For treatment referrals for alcohol or drug use, the SAMHSA National Helpline is free and open 24 hours a day at 1-800-662-HELP (4357). PTSD often travels with depression and anxiety too, and mental.md covers those conditions.

The bottom line

Alcohol offers short relief from PTSD and longer-term harm. It worsens sleep, adds rebound anxiety, feeds avoidance, and makes anger and risky situations more likely. About 45% of adults with PTSD also struggle with alcohol or drugs, so this is common, not a personal failing. You do not need to be sober to start therapy. Integrated treatments like COPE reduce PTSD symptoms and drinking together, and they are available through the VA and many community programs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can alcohol trigger PTSD flashbacks?

Alcohol lowers emotional control and disrupts sleep, both of which make intrusive memories harder to manage. Many people also notice more anxiety and jumpiness the day after drinking, as the body rebounds from alcohol. That state makes reminders of the trauma more likely to set off a strong reaction.

Is it safe to drink while taking an SSRI for PTSD?

No research sets a safe amount of alcohol for people taking PTSD medication. Alcohol disrupts sleep and adds rebound anxiety, which works against what the medicine is meant to do. Ask the prescriber or pharmacist about your specific drug, since the advice differs from one medication to another.

Will my therapist refuse to treat my PTSD if I still drink?

Current VA and Department of Defense guidance says a substance use problem should not keep someone from evidence-based PTSD treatment. Integrated therapies such as COPE are built for people who are still drinking or using. Being honest about your drinking helps the therapist plan sessions safely.

Does cutting back on alcohol improve PTSD symptoms?

Sleep often improves once alcohol is out of the picture, though NIAAA notes full sleep recovery can take 30 or more days. Better sleep tends to ease irritability and concentration problems. Cutting back works best alongside PTSD treatment, since the urge to drink often returns when symptoms spike.

What is the COPE therapy for PTSD and drinking?

COPE stands for Concurrent Treatment of PTSD and Substance Use Disorders Using Prolonged Exposure. It runs about 12 to 13 sessions of 90 minutes and combines exposure therapy for trauma with relapse-prevention skills. Randomized trials in Australian civilians and U.S. veterans found it reduced PTSD symptoms more than substance use treatment alone.
