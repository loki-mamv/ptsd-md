Can PTSD Cause Panic Attacks?

Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Yes. Panic attacks are common in people with PTSD. In a national U.S. survey, 35% of adults with past-year PTSD had a panic attack in the same year. Most of these attacks follow a trauma reminder, so they count as part of PTSD and not as a separate panic disorder. New chest pain still needs emergency care, because a panic attack and a heart attack share symptoms.

Can PTSD really cause panic attacks?

Yes. A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear with strong body symptoms. The National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) lists a pounding or racing heart, sweating, trembling, difficulty breathing, chest pain, and stomach pain or nausea. NIMH also says that an isolated panic attack is not a mental disorder.

PTSD makes these surges more likely. The VA National Center for PTSD describes four symptom types: reliving the event, avoiding reminders, negative thoughts and feelings, and hyperarousal. Its plain-language page does not use the word "panic". But reliving and hyperarousal describe a body that reacts to a reminder as if the danger is back. When that reaction peaks fast and hard, it meets the definition of a panic attack.

The diagnostic manual allows for this. Since DSM-5 in 2013, a panic attack can be listed as a specifier with any DSM-5 disorder, not only the anxiety disorders. A clinician can record "PTSD with panic attacks" as one diagnosis. A 2014 review of the DSM-5 changes describes the same rule.

How common are panic attacks in people with PTSD?

Common, but the number depends on who was counted. These figures come from different samples, so do not compare them directly.

  • General population: A 2010 analysis of the National Comorbidity Survey Replication looked at 203 adults who met DSM-IV criteria for past-year PTSD. Past-year panic attacks were found in 35% of them.
  • People seeking treatment: An older clinic study (Falsetti and Resnick, 1997), as summarized in a 2012 paper, found that 69% of treatment-seeking trauma survivors had at least one panic attack in the two weeks before they came for care.

The treatment-seeking number is higher, which makes sense. People with more severe symptoms are more likely to ask for help. In the national survey, panic attacks were linked to greater PTSD-related disability, less time at work, and more depression and substance use problems.

The overlap also runs across time. A study of 12,467 trauma-exposed U.S. adults found that PTSD was associated with later onset of panic disorder (hazard ratio 1.60). Panic disorder was also associated with later PTSD (hazard ratio 1.21). The link goes both ways, and it is stronger from PTSD to panic.

How is a trauma-cued panic attack different from an unexpected one?

The difference is the cue. An unexpected panic attack has no clear cause. It can start while you rest, read, or sleep. A cued (expected) attack follows a trigger you can name afterward.

In PTSD, most attacks are cued. In the 1997 clinic study cited above, 84% of trauma patients with panic attacks said trauma reminders set them off. The cue can be external, such as a place, an object, or a smell. It can also be internal, such as a fast heartbeat after exercise that feels like the heartbeat during the trauma. Our guide to PTSD triggers covers how these cues form.

The fear inside the attack also differs. A small Australian study compared 22 people with panic disorder and 18 people with PTSD. Both groups feared the body sensations to a similar degree. The PTSD group was more likely to fear the trauma memories and fear being harmed again. In plain terms:

  • Panic disorder: "Something is wrong with my body. I am having a heart attack."
  • PTSD: "It is happening again. I am not safe."

Many people with PTSD have both thoughts in the same attack. The study had only 40 participants, so treat this as a pattern and not a rule.

Is it panic disorder, or panic attacks inside PTSD?

A panic attack is an event. Panic disorder is a diagnosis. NIMH says panic disorder may be diagnosed when a person has recurrent and unexpected panic attacks, along with ongoing worry or behavior changes.

The key word is "unexpected". If every attack follows a trauma reminder, PTSD usually explains it and the clinician adds the panic attack specifier. If attacks also come with no cue, and you start to fear the attacks themselves, a clinician may diagnose panic disorder in addition to PTSD. Signs that point toward a second diagnosis:

  • Attacks that start with no reminder, including during sleep or rest.
  • Constant worry about the next attack, apart from fear of the trauma.
  • Avoiding exercise, caffeine, or crowded places because of the body sensations, not because of a memory.

You do not have to sort this out alone. A clinician does it with a structured interview. The answer matters because it changes the treatment plan. For a wider comparison, see PTSD vs. anxiety disorders, or visit our sister site anxiety.md for more on panic disorder.

When do chest symptoms need emergency care?

Treat new or different chest symptoms as an emergency. Do not assume it is panic. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) lists these heart attack symptoms:

  • Chest pain, heaviness, or discomfort in the center or left side of the chest.
  • Pain in the arms, back, shoulders, neck, jaw, or belly.
  • Shortness of breath, heavy sweating, nausea or vomiting, or lightheadedness.
  • Unusual tiredness, sometimes for days, which is more common in women.

Several of these also occur in a panic attack. You cannot reliably tell the two apart by how they feel. NHLBI's advice is direct: call 9-1-1 for emergency medical care, even if you are not sure that you are having a heart attack.

A first panic attack also deserves a medical visit. NIMH notes that a provider does a physical exam to make sure an unrelated physical problem is not causing the symptoms. If you have thoughts of suicide, call or text 988 in the U.S. Veterans can press 1 after dialing.

How is PTSD with panic attacks treated?

Treatment usually starts with the PTSD. The 2023 VA/DoD clinical practice guideline recommends individual trauma-focused psychotherapy over medication. It names three therapies: Cognitive Processing Therapy, Prolonged Exposure, and Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing. Our comparison of Prolonged Exposure and Cognitive Processing Therapy explains how two of them work.

Three other points from the same guideline apply here:

  • A second disorder is not a barrier. The guideline suggests that a co-occurring disorder should not prevent a person from getting these therapies.
  • Medication is an option. It recommends paroxetine, sertraline, or venlafaxine for PTSD. NIMH lists the same drug classes (SSRIs and SNRIs) for panic disorder. A prescriber sets the dose.
  • Benzodiazepines are not advised. NIMH says they reduce panic symptoms quickly but carry a risk of dependence. The VA/DoD guideline gives a "Strong against" rating to benzodiazepines for PTSD.

For panic itself, NIMH describes cognitive behavioral therapy, including exposure methods, as the main psychotherapy. One method is interoceptive exposure. You bring on the feared body sensations on purpose, in a safe setting, until they lose their threat. The authors of the 12,467-person study suggest that clinicians consider adding it to PTSD treatment.

Researchers have also tested a combined program. Multiple channel exposure therapy joins elements of Cognitive Processing Therapy with panic control treatment. Its developers report preliminary results that it may help people with PTSD and panic attacks. The evidence is early, and the VA/DoD guideline does not list it among its recommended therapies.

The bottom line

PTSD can cause panic attacks, and it often does. About one in three adults with past-year PTSD in a national survey had them. Most are cued by trauma reminders and are recorded as part of PTSD. Attacks with no cue, plus fear of the attacks themselves, can mean panic disorder is also present. Both respond to treatment, and trauma-focused therapy is the usual starting point. Get emergency care for chest symptoms you cannot explain.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a PTSD panic attack feel like?

It feels like a sudden wave of fear with a racing heart, sweating, trembling, and trouble breathing. In PTSD it usually follows a reminder of the trauma. Many people also feel that the event is happening again or that they are in danger.

How do I know if I have panic disorder as well as PTSD?

Panic disorder involves recurrent attacks that come with no clear cue, plus ongoing worry about the next attack. If your attacks only follow trauma reminders, PTSD usually explains them. A clinician can tell the two apart with a structured interview.

Are panic attacks in PTSD dangerous?

A panic attack itself passes, but it shares symptoms with a heart attack. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute says to call 9-1-1 even if you are not sure. In a national survey, panic attacks in people with PTSD were also linked to greater disability, so they are worth treating.

Will treating PTSD stop the panic attacks?

It often helps when trauma reminders cause the attacks, because trauma-focused therapy targets those reminders. No source reviewed for this article gives a rate for how often the attacks stop. Some clinicians add panic-specific methods such as interoceptive exposure when attacks continue.

Are benzodiazepines used for panic attacks in PTSD?

The 2023 VA/DoD guideline recommends against benzodiazepines for PTSD. NIMH notes they reduce panic symptoms quickly but carry a risk of dependence. Talk with your prescriber before you start or stop any medication.
