What Is Hypervigilance and How Do You Calm It Down?

The short answer: hypervigilance is a state of constant, exhausting alertness to threat. It is one of the six arousal and reactivity symptoms in the DSM-5 criteria for PTSD. In the moment, slow breathing and grounding can bring the body down. Over the long term, the strongest evidence points to trauma-focused therapy, because it addresses the reason the alarm keeps going off.

What is hypervigilance?

Hypervigilance is a persistent scanning for danger. The person sits facing the door, tracks every exit, reads every face, and cannot switch it off. It is not paranoia. It is a threat-detection system stuck in the on position.

In the DSM-5, PTSD includes a cluster called "marked alterations in arousal and reactivity." A diagnosis requires two or more of six items in that cluster: irritable behavior and angry outbursts, reckless or self-destructive behavior, hypervigilance, exaggerated startle response, problems with concentration, and sleep disturbance. Symptoms must last more than one month (DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for PTSD, NCBI Bookshelf).

The VA National Center for PTSD describes the same cluster in plain terms: "feeling on edge or keyed up," being jittery, or always alert and on the lookout for danger. Trouble sleeping, poor concentration, and startling at loud noises sit alongside it (National Center for PTSD, PTSD Basics).

PTSD itself is not rare. About 6% of U.S. adults will have PTSD at some point in their lives, and about 5% have it in a given year. Lifetime rates are about 8% in women and 4% in men (National Center for PTSD, How Common Is PTSD in Adults?).

What does hypervigilance feel like?

A few patterns come up again and again:

  • Positioning. Choosing seats with a wall behind you and a clear view of the door.
  • Scanning. Tracking every movement, sound, and shift in someone's tone. Crowds feel like work.
  • Startle. Jumping at a slammed door, then needing minutes to settle.
  • Body tension. Clenched jaw, tight shoulders, a heart rate that never fully slows.
  • Fatigue. Constant monitoring costs energy. Many people are exhausted by evening and still cannot sleep.
  • Irritability. A nervous system braced for attack reacts fast. See PTSD and anger.

Why does the body stay on alert?

The stress response exists to keep you alive. After trauma, it can recalibrate so that the threshold for alarm drops and stays low. Cues that resemble the original event, and sometimes cues that do not, trigger the same physical response. Our guide to PTSD triggers covers this in detail.

This is measurable. A meta-analysis of 58 resting baseline studies and 25 startle studies compared adults with and without PTSD. People with PTSD had higher resting heart rate, a larger heart rate response to startling sounds, and a slower decline in skin conductance across repeated startles. In plain terms, the body reacts more and calms down more slowly (Pole, Psychological Bulletin, 2007).

That slow habituation matters. The body has not finished with one startle before the next arrives. Hypervigilance is what it feels like when the alarm never fully resets. It is the "fight" and "flight" side of the trauma response, described in fight, flight, freeze, and fawn.

How do you calm hypervigilance in the moment?

Two skills have the most support for bringing arousal down quickly. Neither treats PTSD on its own, but both buy time.

Slow breathing

Slow breathing means roughly 6 breaths per minute, with a longer exhale than inhale. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 223 studies found that voluntary slow breathing increased vagally mediated heart rate variability, a marker of parasympathetic activity, during the session, immediately after a single session, and after multi-session training (Laborde et al., Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews, 2022). A separate review of slow breathing under 10 breaths per minute reported reduced symptoms of arousal, anxiety, and anger alongside those autonomic changes (Zaccaro et al., Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 2018).

The evidence in PTSD specifically is smaller and weaker. A 2025 meta-analysis of six randomized trials (517 patients, four of the trials in PTSD) found that yogic breathing reduced symptom severity compared with passive controls, with a small effect size. Four of the six trials had a high risk of bias, and the authors stated that breathing practices should not replace standard treatment (Mütze et al., Frontiers in Psychiatry, 2025).

A practical version: breathe in through the nose for about 4 seconds, out through the mouth for about 6 seconds, for 2 to 5 minutes. Practice it when calm so it is available when you are not.

Grounding

Grounding pulls attention out of threat-scanning and into the present. Common forms include naming five things you can see, four you can hear, and three you can touch; pressing your feet into the floor; or holding something cold. The National Center for PTSD lists breathing exercises, muscle relaxation, stretching, and time in nature among coping tools, notes that they work better with practice, and cautions that in a few people relaxation can increase distress at first (National Center for PTSD, Coping With Traumatic Stress Reactions). Grounding has far less trial evidence than breathing. It is taught because it is simple and low risk, not because large trials have tested it.

What helps over weeks and months?

In-the-moment skills manage the alarm. Trauma-focused therapy turns it down. Every major PTSD guideline gives its strongest recommendation to trauma-focused psychotherapy, and all of them include prolonged exposure (PE), cognitive processing therapy (CPT), and trauma-focused CBT. Four of five guidelines also strongly recommend EMDR; the APA 2017 guideline rated it moderate (Hamblen et al., A Guide to Guidelines for the Treatment of PTSD, 2019; APA Clinical Practice Guideline for PTSD, 2017).

The 2023 VA/DoD guideline names CPT, EMDR, and PE as its recommended therapies. It also gives a weaker "suggested" rating to mindfulness-based stress reduction, present-centered therapy, and written exposure therapy (ISTSS summary of the 2023 VA/DoD guideline).

Hypervigilance is driven by a prediction: the danger is still here. Trauma-focused therapy updates that prediction. As the memory loses its charge, the cues that trigger scanning lose theirs. Our treatment overview compares the main options.

Daily habits support the work. The National Center for PTSD advises daily exercise to reduce body tension, a regular bedtime, and cutting alcohol, tobacco, and caffeine because they harm sleep (National Center for PTSD). If your mind will not switch off at night, insomnia.md covers the anxiety and insomnia cycle, and our guide to PTSD nightmares covers the trauma-specific side.

Do medications help with hypervigilance?

Some do, indirectly. No drug targets hypervigilance alone. The medications with the best evidence treat PTSD as a whole, and arousal symptoms improve with it.

  • SSRIs and SNRIs. The 2023 VA/DoD guideline strongly recommends sertraline, paroxetine, and venlafaxine as first-line medications, and the National Center for PTSD calls them among the most effective treatments for PTSD (National Center for PTSD, Medications for PTSD).
  • Prazosin. The 2023 guideline suggests prazosin for trauma-related nightmares but suggests against it for PTSD in general, because it has not improved overall symptoms (National Center for PTSD, Clinician's Guide to Medications for PTSD).
  • Beta-blockers such as propranolol. These are sometimes proposed because they blunt the physical side of arousal. The 2023 VA/DoD guideline recommends neither for nor against them, stating that available data do not support their use. A 2025 meta-analysis of seven randomized trials found a statistically significant reduction in PTSD symptoms with propranolol, but the authors called the evidence preliminary and asked for more research on long-term safety (Li et al., Frontiers in Pharmacology, 2025).
  • Benzodiazepines. The VA/DoD guideline recommends against them for PTSD. They can interfere with the mental processes needed to benefit from therapy, and a meta-analysis linked them to worse outcomes.

What makes hypervigilance worse?

Avoidance is the big one. Staying home and organizing life around not being startled feel protective. They also confirm the prediction that the world is dangerous, so the alarm never learns otherwise. Caffeine, alcohol, and poor sleep raise baseline arousal. None of this is a moral failing. It is the same threat system doing what it was built to do.

The bottom line

Hypervigilance is a diagnosable PTSD symptom, not a character flaw. The body's alarm reacts more and resets more slowly, and research can measure that. Slow breathing and grounding help in the moment, and they are worth practicing daily. The lasting fix is trauma-focused therapy, backed by every major guideline, with sertraline, paroxetine, or venlafaxine as medication options. If you have been on alert for more than a month and it is interfering with your life, an assessment is the right next step.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hypervigilance a symptom of PTSD?

Yes. In the DSM-5, hypervigilance is one of six items in the arousal and reactivity cluster, alongside irritability or angry outbursts, reckless behavior, exaggerated startle, poor concentration, and sleep disturbance. A PTSD diagnosis requires two or more of these six, plus symptoms from the other clusters, lasting more than one month.

What is the fastest way to calm hypervigilance?

Slow breathing at about 6 breaths per minute, with a longer exhale than inhale, has the most evidence for lowering arousal quickly. A meta-analysis of 223 studies found it raised vagally mediated heart rate variability during and after practice. Grounding exercises, such as naming five things you can see and four you can hear, are widely taught but have less trial evidence. Both work better with regular practice.

Does hypervigilance go away on its own?

It can fade for some people as they recover from trauma, but when it is part of PTSD it often persists without treatment. Trauma-focused therapies such as prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, and EMDR carry the strongest guideline recommendations and address the reason the alarm keeps going off.

Do beta-blockers like propranolol help with hypervigilance?

The evidence is limited. The 2023 VA/DoD guideline recommends neither for nor against beta-blockers for PTSD and states that available data do not support their use. A 2025 meta-analysis of seven randomized trials reported a statistically significant reduction in PTSD symptoms with propranolol, but the authors called the evidence preliminary and asked for more research on long-term safety.

Is hypervigilance the same as paranoia?

No. Paranoia involves fixed false beliefs that others intend harm. Hypervigilance is a heightened, exhausting alertness to possible danger, driven by a threat-detection system that has recalibrated after trauma. Research shows people with PTSD have higher resting heart rate, larger startle responses, and slower habituation to repeated startles, which is a physiological pattern rather than a belief.
