What Medications Are FDA-Approved for PTSD?

Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Only two medications carry FDA approval for PTSD: sertraline (Zoloft) and paroxetine (Paxil). Both are SSRIs, and both were approved on the strength of 12-week placebo-controlled trials. The 2023 VA/DoD guideline recommends those two plus venlafaxine (Effexor), which is used off-label. Guidelines recommend against benzodiazepines, and they rank trauma-focused therapy above any pill.

Which medications does the FDA approve for PTSD?

Two. The National Institute of Mental Health states that the FDA "has approved two selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), a type of antidepressant medication, for the treatment of PTSD." Those two are sertraline and paroxetine.

  • Sertraline (Zoloft): the FDA label on DailyMed says it is "indicated for the treatment of posttraumatic stress disorder in adults."
  • Paroxetine (Paxil): the FDA label on DailyMed lists PTSD among six approved adult indications.

Every other drug you may hear about for PTSD is off-label. That includes venlafaxine, fluoxetine, prazosin and quetiapine. Off-label does not mean useless. It means the manufacturer never asked the FDA to review the drug for PTSD. Some off-label drugs have strong trial data. Others have almost none.

What do the FDA labels actually say?

The sertraline label describes two 12-week flexible-dose studies. Patients started at 25 mg per day for one week and were then dosed between 50 and 200 mg per day. The mean dose for completers was 146 mg and 151 mg per day. Sertraline beat placebo on the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale (CAPS) and on the Impact of Event Scale.

The same label reports two more placebo-controlled trials where the difference "was not statistically significant." One of those was run "in predominantly male veterans." Post hoc analyses found a clear benefit in women but "essentially no effect in the relatively smaller number of men." The label says the meaning of that gender gap "is unknown at this time." A maintenance study followed 96 responders after 24 weeks of open treatment. Those who stayed on sertraline relapsed less over the next 28 weeks than those switched to placebo.

The paroxetine label describes two 12-week trials of 858 combined patients whose PTSD had lasted a mean of 13 years. Study 1 tested fixed doses of 20 mg and 40 mg. Both beat placebo, and the label found no extra benefit from the higher dose. Study 2 used flexible dosing from 20 to 50 mg and also beat placebo. A third study beat placebo on the CAPS score but not on the global improvement rating. The labeled dose range for PTSD is 20 mg to start and 50 mg maximum. Unlike sertraline, the paroxetine trials found no difference in outcome by gender.

What do the VA/DoD and APA guidelines recommend?

The 2023 VA/DoD clinical practice guideline recommends three drugs: paroxetine, sertraline or venlafaxine. The VA National Center for PTSD's clinician guide lists the guideline's dose ranges as sertraline 50 to 200 mg, paroxetine 20 to 60 mg and venlafaxine 75 to 300 mg daily. It notes that only sertraline and paroxetine hold FDA approval for PTSD.

A 2006 trial of 538 outpatients compared venlafaxine ER, sertraline and placebo over 12 weeks. CAPS scores fell 41.8 points on venlafaxine, 39.4 on sertraline and 33.9 on placebo. Remission at week 12 was 30.2% for venlafaxine and 19.6% for placebo. Sertraline did not separate from placebo in that trial.

The American Psychological Association guideline gives conditional recommendations for four drugs: fluoxetine, paroxetine, sertraline and venlafaxine. It found insufficient evidence for risperidone and topiramate. By contrast, it strongly recommends four therapies: cognitive-behavioral therapy, cognitive processing therapy, cognitive therapy and prolonged exposure. Our overview of PTSD treatment options walks through each one.

Can prazosin help with PTSD nightmares?

Maybe, for nightmares only. Prazosin is a blood pressure drug. The 2023 VA/DoD guideline "suggests against" it for overall PTSD symptoms but "suggests" it for PTSD-related nightmares. That split reflects a divided evidence base.

A 2020 meta-analysis of 8 randomized trials pooled 286 prazosin and 289 placebo participants. Prazosin reduced nightmares with a standardized mean difference of 1.13. It did not beat placebo on overall PTSD symptoms or on sleep quality. The largest single trial cut the other way. The 2018 VA cooperative study randomized 304 veterans with chronic PTSD and frequent nightmares to prazosin or placebo for 26 weeks. It found "no significant differences" in nightmare scores, sleep quality or clinical improvement at 10 weeks or 26 weeks.

So prazosin is worth asking about if nightmares are your worst symptom. It is not a treatment for PTSD as a whole. For non-drug approaches, read our guide on whether PTSD nightmares ever go away.

Why are benzodiazepines not recommended for PTSD?

Benzodiazepines such as alprazolam, clonazepam and lorazepam calm anxiety within an hour. That is also why they backfire in PTSD. The 2023 VA/DoD guideline recommends against benzodiazepines outright. A summary from ISTSS notes that benzodiazepines and cannabis are the only two treatments the guideline flags as "strongly contraindicated."

The evidence behind that stance comes from a 2015 systematic review of 18 studies with 5,236 participants. It found benzodiazepines "ineffective for PTSD treatment and prevention." Worse, their use was linked to greater overall PTSD severity, a higher chance of developing PTSD after a recent trauma, poorer psychotherapy outcomes, aggression, depression and substance use. The authors called them "relatively contraindicated" for anyone with PTSD or recent trauma.

A 2024 study of VA records found new benzodiazepine prescriptions among veterans with PTSD fell from 6.6% in 2012 to 1.9% in 2022. The core problem is mechanism. Trauma-focused therapy works by teaching your nervous system that reminders are safe. A drug that blunts fear during those sessions can block that learning.

How long does PTSD medication take to work?

Weeks, not days. The VA's patient page on medications for PTSD says most people "start to feel better in about 4-6 weeks." The FDA trials for both approved drugs ran 12 weeks, so 12 weeks at a working dose is the fair test. Common side effects listed by the VA include upset stomach, sweating, headache, dizziness and sexual problems. In the paroxetine PTSD trials, 11.7% of patients stopped because of an adverse reaction.

Medication has no built-in end date. The sertraline label states that PTSD "requires several months or longer of sustained pharmacological therapy beyond response to initial treatment." The VA says you "will need to keep taking the medication to keep getting the benefits." Stopping also needs care. The paroxetine label describes tapering by 10 mg per week in the trials, because abrupt stops can cause withdrawal symptoms. For a full timeline, see how long PTSD treatment takes.

Does medication work as well as trauma-focused therapy?

No, and every major guideline says so. The 2023 VA/DoD guideline recommends "specific manualized psychotherapies over pharmacotherapy," naming prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy and EMDR. The ISTSS summary gives the reason: "larger and more persistent improvement" with therapy. Medication is the recommended option when those therapies are "not available or feasible," or when you prefer it.

A 2019 network meta-analysis in JAMA Psychiatry pooled 12 trials with 922 participants that directly compared drugs, therapy or both. At the end of treatment, no approach was clearly better. At long-term follow-up, psychotherapy beat medication with a standardized mean difference of 0.83. Combined treatment also beat medication alone. Skills learned in therapy persist. A drug's effect stops when the drug does.

That does not make medication a bad choice. It helps when therapy waitlists are long or when someone is not ready to face trauma memories. Many people with PTSD also have depression, and an SSRI can treat both. depression.md covers that overlap in detail.

The bottom line

Sertraline and paroxetine are the only FDA-approved PTSD medications, each backed by 12-week trials and a labeled dose range. Venlafaxine is off-label but carries an equal recommendation in the 2023 VA/DoD guideline. Prazosin may ease nightmares but does not treat PTSD as a whole. Benzodiazepines are recommended against because they do not work and can make PTSD worse. Give any SSRI or SNRI 4 to 6 weeks to start working and 12 weeks for a fair trial. If you can get prolonged exposure, CPT or EMDR, guidelines say to start there.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is venlafaxine (Effexor) approved by the FDA for PTSD?

No. Venlafaxine is used off-label for PTSD. The 2023 VA/DoD guideline still recommends it alongside sertraline and paroxetine, based on trial data. A 12-week trial of 538 patients found venlafaxine ER lowered PTSD scores more than placebo, with 30.2% reaching remission versus 19.6% on placebo.

Can I take PTSD medication and do trauma-focused therapy at the same time?

Yes. NIMH notes that providers may prescribe SSRIs along with psychotherapy. A 2019 network meta-analysis found combined treatment beat medication alone at long-term follow-up. The one exception is benzodiazepines, which guidelines say can interfere with the benefits of therapy.

Does sertraline work for men with PTSD?

The evidence is weaker for men. The FDA label reports that post hoc analyses found a clear benefit in women but essentially no effect in the smaller number of men studied, including one trial in mostly male veterans. The label says the clinical meaning of that gap is unknown. Paroxetine trials found no difference by gender.

Is fluoxetine (Prozac) used for PTSD?

Sometimes, off-label. The American Psychological Association guideline gives fluoxetine a conditional recommendation for PTSD, the same level as sertraline, paroxetine and venlafaxine. It is not FDA-approved for PTSD and is not among the three drugs the 2023 VA/DoD guideline recommends.

What happens if I stop paroxetine or sertraline suddenly?

Both labels warn against abrupt stops. Symptoms can include dizziness, sensory disturbances, anxiety, irritability and sleep problems. In the paroxetine PTSD trials, the dose was lowered by 10 mg per week before stopping. Work out a taper plan with your prescriber rather than stopping on your own.
