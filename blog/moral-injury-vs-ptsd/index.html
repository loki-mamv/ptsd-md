What Is Moral Injury and How Is It Different From PTSD?

The short answer: Moral injury is lasting distress after you do, fail to prevent, or witness something that violates your deep moral beliefs. It is not a formal diagnosis. PTSD is a diagnosis that starts with exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence. The two overlap a lot, especially in guilt and shame, but you can have either one without the other.

What exactly is moral injury?

The research definition most people use comes from a 2009 paper by Brett Litz and colleagues at the VA National Center for PTSD. They described "potentially morally injurious events" as perpetrating, failing to prevent, or bearing witness to acts that go against deeply held moral beliefs and expectations. Moral injury is the long-term harm that can follow. That harm can be emotional, psychological, behavioral, spiritual, and social.

Litz did not invent the term. Clinicians who write about moral injury credit both Litz (2009) and Jonathan Shay (1994), as this 2014 therapy paper notes. Shay's work came 15 years earlier.

The National Center for PTSD lists guilt, shame, betrayal, disgust, and anger as hallmark reactions. It separates two of them clearly:

  • Guilt is about the act: "I did something bad."
  • Shame spreads to the whole self: "I am bad because of what I did."

An inability to forgive yourself is also common. The VA notes this can show up as self-sabotage, such as ending a relationship because you feel unworthy of love. Some people also question their faith or their relationship with a Higher Power.

Is moral injury a mental health diagnosis?

No. The VA's patient guide says moral injury can occur together with PTSD and depression, "but it is not a diagnosis itself." You will not find it as a disorder in the DSM-5-TR.

The science is still young. A 2019 integrative review of 116 studies listed key gaps. There was no consensus definition and no gold-standard measure. Most research focused on the military, and clinical trials were small.

That is starting to change. Researchers now have validated questionnaires, such as the Moral Injury Outcomes Scale and the Moral Injury and Distress Scale. A mental health provider or a chaplain can use tools like these, or a conversation, to look for it.

How is moral injury different from PTSD?

The clearest difference is the starting event. PTSD under DSM-5 requires exposure to death, threatened death, actual or threatened serious injury, or actual or threatened sexual violence. Moral injury requires that you feel a moral line was crossed, by you or by someone else. Many events do both. Some do not. A health care worker forced to ration care may not have faced any threat to life.

The symptoms differ too. PTSD includes hyperarousal: feeling on high alert, jumpy, or unable to sleep. The VA says this is "not usually a feature of moral injury." Our guide to PTSD symptoms explains the rest of the picture.

You will often read that PTSD is about fear and moral injury is about guilt and shame. That is a useful shorthand, but it is too simple. DSM-5 dropped the old rule that a person must react with intense fear or horror. It also lists exaggerated blame of self or others as a PTSD symptom. So guilt and shame are part of PTSD, not only moral injury.

The emotional profile can still differ by event type. The VA cites a study that found perpetration events were linked to more re-experiencing, guilt, and self-blame than life-threatening events.

What happens when someone has both?

Having both is linked to worse outcomes. According to the National Center for PTSD, people with moral injury and PTSD tend to have more severe PTSD and depression symptoms. They are also more likely to have suicidal intent and behaviors.

A 2018 meta-analysis in the British Journal of Psychiatry pooled 13 studies with 6,373 participants. Work-related morally injurious events accounted for 9.4% of the variance in PTSD, 5.2% in depression, and 2.0% in suicidality. The authors called these conclusions tentative, since few high-quality studies existed.

Depression and moral injury share a lot of ground: guilt, worthlessness, and withdrawal. depression.md has more on depression itself. If you are thinking about suicide, call or text 988 now.

Who is most at risk of moral injury?

Early research focused on service members and veterans. The VA lists examples such as killing or harming others, making decisions that affect others' survival, being unable to care for all the wounded, and freezing during a dangerous event. Our article on PTSD in veterans covers the wider picture.

Moral injury is not only a military problem. The VA notes it in health care workers who must decide who gets care first or how to divide scarce resources. It is also studied in first responders, law enforcement, refugees, and civilians exposed to community violence.

A nationally representative U.S. survey of 1,232 people, published in 2025, measured this directly:

  • Exposure to a potentially morally injurious event: 49.3% of combat veterans, 50.8% of health care workers, and 41.6% of first responders.
  • Clinically meaningful moral injury: 6.5% of combat veterans, 7.3% of health care workers, and 4.1% of first responders.

Veterans were more likely to report violating their values through their own actions. Health care workers were more likely to report witnessing others' wrongful acts. Exposure is common, but most people do not develop lasting moral injury. The VA says most people feel better over time.

What treatments are being studied?

The VA states that no medications are known to help moral injury. Treatment is talk therapy, and the research is still in progress.

Standard PTSD therapies. Prolonged exposure and cognitive processing therapy can target guilt, shame, and beliefs like "I am unforgivable." The VA says some studies found they reduce trauma-related guilt and shame. One study found guilt often remained. The VA patient page adds that PTSD treatments still work for PTSD when moral injury is also present.

Therapies built for moral injury. These are under study, mostly with service members and veterans:

  • Trauma-Informed Guilt Reduction Therapy (TrIGR). Six individual sessions. In a randomized trial of 145 post-9/11 veterans, it reduced guilt more than supportive therapy. PTSD diagnosis was lost in 50% of the TrIGR group versus 14% of the supportive therapy group.
  • Impact of Killing. A therapy given after PTSD treatment, focused on self-forgiveness and making amends. A 2017 pilot trial of 33 combat veterans found better PTSD symptoms and quality of life than a waitlist.
  • Adaptive Disclosure. Twelve individual sessions that include an imagined talk with a compassionate moral authority.
  • Acceptance and Commitment Therapy for Moral Injury (ACT-MI). Focuses on living by your values while carrying moral pain.
  • Building Spiritual Strength. An eight-session group that a chaplain can lead, focused on faith and forgiveness.

Shame makes these events hard to talk about. The VA notes that some people test a therapist with a less painful memory first. Therapists are trained to listen without judgment.

The bottom line

Moral injury is the lasting wound of crossing, or seeing someone cross, a moral line. It is not a diagnosis, and it does not require a life threat. PTSD does. The two share guilt, shame, and broken trust, and having both raises the risk of severe symptoms. Trauma-focused therapy helps many people, and newer therapies aimed at guilt and forgiveness show early promise. If guilt or shame from an event still shapes your life, a mental health provider or chaplain is a good place to start.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you have moral injury without PTSD?

Yes. The VA National Center for PTSD says it is possible to have moral injury and not meet the criteria for PTSD. For example, a health care worker who had to ration care may feel deep guilt without any threat to their own life or any hyperarousal symptoms.

Is moral injury the same as survivor's guilt?

Not exactly. Survivor's guilt can be one form of moral injury, and the VA lists guilt about surviving when others die as a possible source in health care workers. Moral injury is broader. It also covers acts you committed, failed to prevent, or saw others commit.

What is the difference between moral distress and moral injury?

Moral distress is the discomfort people often feel during or after an ethically hard situation. Moral injury is the more lasting harm, when guilt, shame, anger, or loss of trust continue to affect work, relationships, or faith. Researchers now use scales with cut-offs to separate the two.

Can a chaplain help with moral injury?

Yes, for many people. The VA says a chaplain or a mental health provider can help assess moral injury, and some studied treatments, such as Building Spiritual Strength, can be led by chaplains. A licensed therapist is still important if PTSD, depression, or suicidal thoughts are present.

Is there a medication for moral injury?

Not at this time. The VA National Center for PTSD says no medications are known to help moral injury itself. Medications may still be part of care for related conditions such as PTSD or depression, which a prescriber can discuss with you.
