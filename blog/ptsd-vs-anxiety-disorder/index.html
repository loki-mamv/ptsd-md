What Is the Difference Between PTSD and an Anxiety Disorder?

Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: PTSD grows out of a specific traumatic event, while an anxiety disorder needs no trigger event at all. That is why DSM-5 moved PTSD out of the anxiety disorders chapter in 2013 and into a new chapter of trauma- and stressor-related disorders. The two share fear, avoidance and tension, but they respond to different first-line treatments. Getting the label right changes what therapy you should ask for.

Was PTSD ever classified as an anxiety disorder?

Yes. Under DSM-IV, PTSD sat in the anxiety disorders chapter next to panic disorder and phobias. That changed with DSM-5 in 2013. The National Center for PTSD states: "PTSD was included in a new category in DSM-5, Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders." The 2022 text revision (DSM-5-TR) made no changes to the adult PTSD criteria.

The World Health Organization made a similar move. ICD-11, which took effect on 1 January 2022, places PTSD (code 6B40) in a grouping called "disorders specifically associated with stress." A 2022 review by Maercker and Eberle describes the members of that group: PTSD, complex PTSD, prolonged grief disorder, adjustment disorder, and two childhood attachment diagnoses. For every disorder in the group, an identifiable stressor is a necessary, though not sufficient, cause.

So both major diagnostic systems now treat PTSD as a stress-response disorder, not a subtype of anxiety. Obsessive-compulsive disorder was also moved out of the anxiety chapter in DSM-5, into its own category.

What actually separates PTSD from an anxiety disorder?

The clearest dividing line is the event. PTSD cannot be diagnosed without one. DSM-5 Criterion A requires exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence. Anxiety disorders such as generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), panic disorder, social anxiety disorder and specific phobias have no such requirement.

The second line is what the symptoms point at. In PTSD, the symptoms stay tied to the trauma. DSM-5 groups them into four clusters:

  • Re-experiencing: intrusive memories, nightmares and flashbacks of the event.
  • Avoidance: steering clear of reminders, places, people or thoughts linked to the trauma.
  • Negative changes in thinking and mood: at least two, such as blame, shame, or feeling cut off from others.
  • Changes in arousal and reactivity: at least two, such as hypervigilance, a strong startle or poor sleep.

Symptoms must last more than one month and cause real problems in daily life. ICD-11 narrows the core to three clusters: re-experiencing in the here and now, avoidance, and a persistent sense of current threat.

Anxiety disorders look different in shape. GAD centers on broad, hard-to-control worry about many everyday things. Panic disorder centers on sudden panic attacks and fear of the next one. Social anxiety centers on fear of judgment. The fear is real, but it is not anchored to a memory that keeps replaying. Our guide to PTSD symptoms covers each cluster in more detail.

Where do the symptoms overlap?

The overlap is large, which is why the two get confused. Both can bring a racing heart, muscle tension, trouble sleeping, irritability and poor concentration. Both involve avoidance. A person with panic disorder may avoid crowded stores. A person with PTSD may avoid the road where a crash happened.

A few questions help sort them out:

  • Did the symptoms start after a specific frightening event?
  • Do you relive that event through unwanted memories, nightmares or flashbacks?
  • Is your avoidance aimed at reminders of that event, or at many situations with no shared link?
  • Do you feel guilt, shame or detachment tied to what happened?

Yes answers to the first two point toward a trauma-related condition. Only a clinician can make the diagnosis, and a structured interview is the standard way to do it.

How common is each one?

Anxiety disorders are far more common. The National Institute of Mental Health estimates that 19.1% of US adults had any anxiety disorder in the past year. About 31.1% will have one at some point in their lives. Past-year rates were higher in women (23.4%) than in men (14.3%).

For PTSD, NIMH figures put past-year prevalence at 3.6% of US adults. That rate was 5.2% in women and 1.8% in men. Lifetime prevalence was 6.8%.

One catch sits inside those numbers. They come from the National Comorbidity Survey Replication, run from 2001 to 2003 using DSM-IV. NIMH notes that its "any anxiety disorder" figure still counts PTSD and OCD. In its words, "the current DSM-5 no longer places post-traumatic stress disorder or obsessive compulsive disorder in the anxiety disorder category." If you see an older statistic that lists PTSD as an anxiety disorder, this is usually the reason.

Why does the distinction change treatment?

This is where the label matters most. First-line therapy for PTSD targets the trauma memory itself. The National Center for PTSD says: "The talk therapies that work best are: Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT), Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR), and Prolonged Exposure (PE)." These usually run 8 to 16 sessions. Our comparison of prolonged exposure and cognitive processing therapy explains how they differ.

For GAD and panic disorder, the core therapy is cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) aimed at worry, panic cycles and feared situations. The World Health Organization's 2023 mhGAP recommendations advise brief structured psychological interventions based on CBT principles for adults with GAD or panic disorder. They also recommend SSRIs and stress management techniques such as relaxation or mindfulness training.

The two tracks also differ in how well standard CBT works. A 2018 meta-analysis of 41 placebo-controlled trials found large effects of CBT for GAD and small to moderate effects for PTSD and panic disorder. In the PTSD trials, dropout was higher with CBT (29.0%) than with placebo (17.2%). The authors concluded that more effective treatments are especially needed for PTSD.

Medication rules diverge too. The VA lists paroxetine, sertraline and venlafaxine as the PTSD medicines with the most evidence. Benzodiazepines show the sharpest split. The VA notes they are "recommended for short-term treatment of severe anxiety, panic, or insomnia." For PTSD, the VA advises against them. "If you use benzodiazepines to escape reminders of your trauma, you never truly learn to manage your stress. This makes it harder to recover from PTSD."

A PTSD case treated only as general anxiety may get a benzodiazepine and worry-focused therapy. Neither targets the trauma memory that keeps the symptoms going.

Can you have PTSD and an anxiety disorder at the same time?

Yes, and it is common. The original National Comorbidity Survey analysis of PTSD found that PTSD "is strongly comorbid with other lifetime DSM-III-R disorders." It also reported that more than one third of people with an episode of PTSD had not recovered even after many years.

When both are present, clinicians usually decide which problem drives the most distress and treat that first. Panic attacks that only happen around trauma reminders are often part of the PTSD. Panic that shows up anywhere, with no link to the event, may be a separate panic disorder. If general worry or panic is the main issue, anxiety.md covers those conditions in depth.

The bottom line

PTSD and anxiety disorders share fear, tension and avoidance. What sets PTSD apart is a traumatic event and symptoms that keep circling back to it. DSM-5 and ICD-11 both reflect that by classing PTSD as a stress-related disorder. The practical payoff is treatment: trauma-focused therapy such as CPT, PE or EMDR for PTSD, and CBT aimed at worry or panic for anxiety disorders.

If your symptoms began after a frightening event, say so plainly when you see a clinician. It can change the whole treatment plan.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is PTSD still considered an anxiety disorder?

No. DSM-5 moved PTSD into a trauma- and stressor-related disorders chapter in 2013, and ICD-11 places it among disorders specifically associated with stress. Some older statistics, including NIMH's any-anxiety-disorder figures, still count PTSD because they used DSM-IV.

Can anxiety turn into PTSD?

Anxiety on its own does not become PTSD. PTSD requires exposure to a traumatic event, such as threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence. A person with an existing anxiety disorder can later develop PTSD after a trauma, and the two can then exist side by side.

Are panic attacks a sign of PTSD or panic disorder?

They can be part of either. Panic that happens mainly around reminders of a trauma often belongs to PTSD. Panic that strikes in many settings with no link to a past event points more toward panic disorder, and a clinician can sort out which fits.

Do the same medicines treat PTSD and anxiety?

There is some overlap, since SSRIs are used for both. The VA lists paroxetine, sertraline and venlafaxine as the PTSD medicines with the most evidence. Benzodiazepines differ: the VA says they can help short-term severe anxiety or panic but advises against them for PTSD.

Why does the correct diagnosis matter so much?

The best-supported therapies differ. PTSD responds best to trauma-focused therapies such as CPT, prolonged exposure and EMDR, while anxiety disorders are usually treated with CBT aimed at worry or panic. A missed PTSD diagnosis can mean years of therapy that never addresses the trauma.
