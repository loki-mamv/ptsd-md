Does a Stellate Ganglion Block (SGB) Work for PTSD?

October 17, 2026 5 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: A stellate ganglion block (SGB) may ease some PTSD symptoms, but the evidence is mixed. One sham-controlled trial in 113 service members found a modest benefit at 8 weeks, while an earlier trial found no difference from sham. The 2023 VA/DoD guideline says the evidence is insufficient to recommend for or against it, and its use for PTSD is off-label.

What is a stellate ganglion block?

The stellate ganglion is a cluster of sympathetic nerves in the lower front of the neck. These nerves are part of the system that drives the "fight or flight" response. In an SGB, a clinician injects a local anesthetic next to this nerve cluster, often with ultrasound or X-ray guidance.

A 2024 analysis in Translational Psychiatry describes the goal as temporarily inhibiting sympathetic nervous activity. SGB was first used for pain conditions of the head, face, neck, and arm. A 2021 systematic review in Chronic Stress found that most psychiatric studies used 1 to 2 right-sided blocks with 0.5% ropivacaine.

What did the 2020 randomized trial find?

The largest published sham-controlled trial appeared in JAMA Psychiatry in 2020 (Rae Olmsted and colleagues). It ran from May 2016 to March 2018 at 3 US Army pain management centers.

  • Who: 113 active-duty service members (100 men, 13 women) with PTSD symptoms, on stable psychiatric medication.
  • Design: 74 received two right-sided SGBs 2 weeks apart. 39 received a sham injection of saline into the neck muscles.
  • Result: On the clinician-rated CAPS-5 scale (0 to 80), scores fell by 12.6 points with SGB and 6.1 points with sham at 8 weeks (P = .01).

That is a difference of about 6.5 points in favor of SGB. Baseline scores were mild to moderate, about 38 to 40 out of 80. The full article reports 6 adverse events, none serious.

The authors themselves named limits. Follow-up lasted only 8 weeks. The doctors doing the injections knew which treatment they gave. The active block often causes a droopy eyelid and small pupil (Horner syndrome), which could tell some participants they got the real drug. The authors concluded SGB "merits further trials as a PTSD treatment adjunct."

A secondary analysis of the same trial found the largest gains in the arousal and reactivity cluster. Those gains came mostly from less hypervigilance, better concentration, and better sleep.

Did other trials agree?

No, not all of them. An earlier 2016 double-blind trial in Regional Anesthesia and Pain Medicine (Hanling and colleagues) tested a single SGB against sham in military service members. PTSD, anxiety, and depression scores improved over time in both groups. The trial found "no statistically or clinically relevant difference" between them.

Newer trials have tested SGB as an add-on to talk therapy:

  • A 2025 open-label trial in Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics assigned 86 military personnel and veterans to SGB before or after daily cognitive processing therapy (CPT). Both groups improved. Symptoms dropped faster when SGB came first. There was no sham, so placebo effects cannot be ruled out.
  • A 2026 placebo-controlled pilot in the Journal of Traumatic Stress randomized 39 adults to SGB or saline before 1 week of intensive CPT. Both groups improved a great deal. SGB "did not produce significantly larger symptom reductions" than placebo.

A 2025 meta-analysis in Autonomic Neuroscience pooled only 2 randomized trials and 1 case-control study. It found CAPS scores 6.24 points lower with SGB than control. The authors said more high-quality trials are needed.

What do VA and DoD guidelines say?

The 2023 VA/DoD clinical practice guideline for PTSD lists SGB in Recommendation 24. It states there is "insufficient evidence to recommend for or against" SGB, along with several other somatic therapies such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation.

The guideline weighed both trials. It flagged two problems with the 2020 trial: the sham may have let participants and staff guess their group, and the follow-up was "too brief" to show how long benefits last. Taken with the negative 2016 trial, the work group made no recommendation either way.

The same guideline does recommend trauma-focused therapies, such as prolonged exposure and cognitive processing therapy, as first-line care. SGB is not a substitute for them.

Is SGB FDA-approved for PTSD?

No. SGB is a procedure that uses local anesthetics approved for other purposes. The FDA label for ropivacaine (Naropin) covers anesthesia for surgery and acute pain management. Using it to treat PTSD is off-label.

Only two drugs, sertraline and paroxetine, carry an FDA indication for PTSD. Our guide to FDA-approved medications for PTSD covers those options.

What are the risks and side effects?

In trials, serious problems were rare. Still, SGB is a needle procedure close to major blood vessels and nerves. A working block often causes a temporary droopy eyelid, small pupil, and red eye on the injected side. This is called Horner syndrome, and the 2020 trial warned all participants to expect it.

The ropivacaine label adds specific warnings. It says that small doses injected into the head and neck area "may produce adverse reactions similar to systemic toxicity" seen with larger doses given into a vein. It also warns that accidental injection into a blood vessel may cause heart rhythm problems or cardiac arrest. For this reason, the label says local anesthetics should only be given by clinicians trained to manage these emergencies.

Ask any clinic about its imaging guidance, its emergency equipment, and who does the injection.

The bottom line

SGB is a promising but unproven PTSD treatment. One sham-controlled trial showed a modest benefit over 8 weeks, another showed none, and add-on studies with talk therapy are mixed. A larger VA-led multisite trial in veterans (NCT05169190) enrolled 410 people and finished primary data collection in September 2025, so better answers may come soon.

If you are thinking about SGB, treat it as a possible add-on, not a replacement for trauma-focused therapy. PTSD often travels with low mood and poor sleep, and insomnia.md covers the sleep side in depth. For service members and veterans, our page on PTSD in veterans covers other options.

Frequently Asked Questions

How fast does a stellate ganglion block work for PTSD?

Some people report changes within minutes to days. A 2021 systematic review noted improvements within 5 minutes in some studies. Controlled trials measured results at weeks, not minutes, and fast early relief has not been proven to last.

How long do the effects of SGB last?

No one knows for sure. The main sham-controlled trial followed people for only 8 weeks. The VA/DoD guideline called that follow-up too brief to judge durability, and some people get repeat blocks.

Can SGB be combined with therapy?

Yes, and that is how many researchers now study it. A 2025 open-label trial found faster symptom drops when SGB came before cognitive processing therapy. A 2026 placebo-controlled pilot found no extra benefit over a saline injection when both groups got intensive therapy.

Does the VA offer stellate ganglion block for PTSD?

Researchers describe SGB as gaining popularity in military settings, but the 2023 VA/DoD guideline makes no recommendation for or against it. A large VA-led trial in veterans has finished enrollment. Ask your VA mental health team what is available at your site.

Who should not get a stellate ganglion block?

People with an allergy to amide-type local anesthetics should not get ropivacaine, according to its FDA label. Trials also excluded people with recent suicidal thoughts, moderate or severe brain injury, and some other conditions. A clinician should review your health history before the procedure.
