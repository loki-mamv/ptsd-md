Is Sleep Paralysis a Symptom of PTSD?

October 5, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida
Table of Contents

The short answer: no, not officially. Sleep paralysis is not one of the 20 symptoms in the DSM-5-TR criteria for PTSD. The only sleep symptoms on that list are nightmares and difficulty sleeping. But sleep paralysis is clearly more common in people with PTSD and trauma histories than in the general population, so the two often travel together.

That distinction matters. An episode of sleep paralysis does not mean you have PTSD. And if you do have PTSD, frequent episodes are worth mentioning to a clinician, because they point to disrupted sleep that can be treated.

What is sleep paralysis?

Sleep paralysis is a brief period when you are awake and aware but cannot move or speak. It happens right as you fall asleep or right as you wake up. MedlinePlus describes episodes as lasting from a few seconds to 1 or 2 minutes, and ending on their own or when someone touches you.

The mechanism is a timing error. During REM sleep, the brain switches off most muscle tone, which stops you from acting out dreams. In sleep paralysis, that muscle shutdown carries over into wakefulness. A clinical review in Neuropsychiatric Disease and Treatment describes it as REM-based atonia combined with conscious awareness.

Many people also have vivid, dream-like hallucinations during an episode. Common reports include a presence in the room, pressure on the chest, and a feeling of suffocation. The same review notes that eye movements stay intact and that the experience is usually frightening.

Is sleep paralysis in the PTSD diagnostic criteria?

No. The VA National Center for PTSD summary of the DSM-5 criteria lists 20 symptoms across four clusters. Two of them concern sleep:

  • Nightmares, under Criterion B (intrusion symptoms).
  • Difficulty sleeping, under Criterion E (arousal and reactivity).

Sleep paralysis does not appear anywhere on the list. The VA page also confirms that the DSM-5-TR, published in March 2022, made no changes to the PTSD criteria for adults. Our guide to the four PTSD symptom clusters walks through the full list.

So a clinician cannot count sleep paralysis toward a PTSD diagnosis. It is better understood as an associated feature: something that shows up more often alongside PTSD without being part of its definition.

How much more common is sleep paralysis in people with PTSD?

Start with the baseline. A systematic review of 35 studies and 36,533 people found that 7.6% of the general population had at least one episode in their lifetime. The figure was 28.3% in students and 31.9% in psychiatric patients.

The link to trauma is consistent across studies. A 2018 systematic review of 42 studies concluded that sleep paralysis "appears to be particularly prevalent in post-traumatic stress disorder," and to a lesser degree in panic disorder. Individual studies show how wide the range is:

  • Cambodian refugees at a psychiatric clinic (100 patients): 65% of those with PTSD had monthly episodes, compared with about 15% of those without PTSD. Higher PTSD severity scores tracked with more episodes (Hinton and colleagues, 2005).
  • Professional firefighters (831 people): probable PTSD was associated with 1.86 times the odds of sleep paralysis (Wróbel-Knybel and colleagues, 2021).
  • Primary care patients (441 adults, 95% African American): 14% reported recent sleep paralysis. It was strongly associated with trauma history, but not with any specific psychiatric diagnosis other than past substance use disorders (Mellman and colleagues, 2008).

Read those numbers with care. The refugee study is small and comes from a single clinic with very high trauma exposure. The firefighter result is modest, and its confidence interval (1.04 to 3.33) nearly touches no effect. The primary care study suggests the link may be with trauma and distress in general, not with the PTSD diagnosis itself.

Why would trauma lead to sleep paralysis?

Nobody has proved that it does. The studies above are cross-sectional, so they show association, not cause. The clinical review cited earlier says it is "currently unclear" whether PTSD causes sleep paralysis, or whether the sleep disruption and hypervigilance that come with PTSD explain the link.

The second explanation is plausible. MedlinePlus lists the known triggers for sleep paralysis: not getting enough sleep, an irregular sleep schedule, mental stress, and sleeping on your back. PTSD produces the first three reliably. Broken sleep means more awakenings, and more chances to wake into REM.

Avoidance can add to it. People with trauma nightmares often delay bedtime or drink to fall asleep, and both habits fragment sleep further. We cover that loop in do PTSD nightmares ever go away?

There is also a risk of confusion between the two experiences. A hallucinated intruder during paralysis can feel like a nightmare or a flashback, especially if it echoes the trauma. The difference is timing: sleep paralysis hallucinations occur only at the edge of sleep, while you are awake and unable to move. See can PTSD cause psychosis or hallucinations? for how clinicians tell these apart.

What else could be causing it?

Most sleep paralysis is "isolated," which means it occurs without another sleep disorder behind it. When episodes repeat and cause real distress, sleep specialists can diagnose recurrent isolated sleep paralysis. The International Classification of Sleep Disorders, third edition (ICSD-3) recognizes it as a formal diagnosis. The DSM-5 has no specific code for it.

The main condition to rule out is narcolepsy. MedlinePlus lists four main narcolepsy symptoms: extreme daytime sleepiness, cataplexy (sudden loss of muscle control while awake), hallucinations at the edges of sleep, and sleep paralysis. If paralysis comes with the first two, a sleep study and a multiple sleep latency test can settle the question.

MedlinePlus also links sleep paralysis to some medicines, including those for ADHD, to substance use, and to bipolar disorder and panic disorder. A clinician will ask about all of these. Poor sleep from any cause raises the risk, and our sister site insomnia.md covers the common ones.

What helps reduce episodes?

There are no large, well-controlled trials of any treatment for sleep paralysis. The options below come from clinical reviews and MedlinePlus, and the evidence behind them is limited.

  • Reassurance. Knowing that an episode is a harmless REM timing error, and that it ends on its own, lowers the fear. Many people never need more than this.
  • Regular sleep. Go to bed and wake up at the same time each day, and get enough sleep. Avoid alcohol and caffeine before bed.
  • Sleep position. Episodes are most likely when sleeping on the back, so side sleeping is commonly recommended.
  • Therapy. A five-session cognitive behavioral therapy manual for isolated sleep paralysis exists. Its authors state that it has not yet been empirically validated.
  • Medication. Some REM-suppressing antidepressants have been reported to reduce episodes. No drug is FDA-approved for sleep paralysis, and this is a decision for a prescriber.

If PTSD is driving the poor sleep, treating the PTSD is the logical first step. The reviews cited here do not report a controlled trial showing that trauma-focused therapy reduces sleep paralysis specifically, so that remains a reasonable expectation and not a proven result.

MedlinePlus advises contacting a provider if you have repeated episodes. Go sooner if you also have daytime sleep attacks, sudden muscle weakness with strong emotion, or fear of sleep that is cutting your sleep short.

The bottom line

Sleep paralysis is not a symptom of PTSD under the DSM-5-TR. Nightmares and difficulty sleeping are. But sleep paralysis is more common in people with PTSD and trauma exposure, most likely because trauma disrupts sleep. About 7.6% of people have at least one episode in their lifetime, and most of them do not have PTSD. Repeated or frightening episodes deserve a conversation with a clinician, who can check for narcolepsy and treat the sleep problem underneath.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does having sleep paralysis mean I have PTSD?

No. A systematic review of 35 studies found that 7.6% of the general population has at least one episode in their lifetime. Sleep paralysis is not part of the PTSD diagnostic criteria, and most people who have it do not have PTSD.

Is sleep paralysis dangerous?

Episodes are frightening but they end on their own. MedlinePlus describes them as lasting from a few seconds to 1 or 2 minutes. Repeated episodes are worth reporting to a provider, because they can be a sign of narcolepsy or another sleep problem.

How is sleep paralysis different from a PTSD nightmare?

A nightmare happens during sleep, and you wake up from it. In sleep paralysis you are already awake and aware, but you cannot move or speak. Hallucinations during sleep paralysis occur only as you fall asleep or wake up.

Can stress alone trigger sleep paralysis?

Mental stress is one of the triggers MedlinePlus lists, along with not getting enough sleep, an irregular sleep schedule, and sleeping on your back. Stress and poor sleep often occur together, so it is hard to separate their effects.

Is there a medication for sleep paralysis?

No drug is FDA-approved for sleep paralysis. Clinical reviews report that some antidepressants that suppress REM sleep have reduced episodes, but large controlled trials are lacking. A prescriber should make that decision.
