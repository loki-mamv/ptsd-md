How Do You Support A Partner Who Has PTSD?

September 13, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Learn how PTSD works, listen without pushing, and back your partner's treatment rather than organizing your life around their symptoms. Well-meant accommodation, such as avoiding every reminder for them, is linked to worse outcomes for both of you. A 15-session couple therapy called Cognitive-Behavioral Conjoint Therapy (CBCT) for PTSD has randomized trial evidence. Look after your own health too, and call or text 988 in a crisis.

How does PTSD show up in a relationship?

PTSD rarely stays inside one person. The VA National Center for PTSD says partners and children may feel more stress and find it harder to talk. It also notes that the quality of a marriage or partnership is often rated lower when a partner has PTSD.

The VA groups the symptoms that touch families into four types. Each one has a typical look at home:

  • Reliving the event. Nightmares, sudden distress, or pulling away after a reminder. Our guide to PTSD triggers explains why a smell or a sound can set this off.
  • Avoidance. Skipping crowds, restaurants, family events, or conversations about the past.
  • Numbness and negative thoughts. Seeming distant, losing interest in sex or shared plans, or saying no one can be trusted.
  • Hyperarousal. Irritability, trouble sleeping, and scanning for danger. See hypervigilance in PTSD for what that feels like from the inside.

The VA also reports that PTSD can affect sexual function and satisfaction. Its clinician guidance summarizes research finding that PTSD in one partner is linked to distress in the other, with a small-to-moderate effect. That distress includes general distress, secondary traumatic stress, and caregiver burden.

None of this means the relationship is failing. It means the symptoms are doing what PTSD symptoms do.

What actually helps day to day?

The VA's advice for helping a family member with PTSD is practical and specific:

  • Learn about PTSD. Knowing the symptoms makes withdrawal or irritability less personal.
  • Tell your partner you want to listen. Let them decide how much to share. You do not need details of the trauma to help.
  • Do not give advice unless asked. Unrequested fixes can feel like pressure.
  • Use "I" statements. The VA notes that "you" statements can sound accusing. "I felt alone at dinner" lands better than "You shut me out."
  • Offer to go to appointments. Being present can make starting or staying in treatment easier.
  • Plan ordinary time together. Dinner, a movie, a walk, or exercise keeps the relationship from becoming only about PTSD.
  • Encourage other support. Contact with friends and family spreads the load beyond you.

Your partner may not want help at first. The VA points out that withdrawal can itself be a symptom. Keep the door open without forcing it.

The strongest form of support is encouraging effective treatment. Trauma-focused therapies work for many people. Prolonged exposure and cognitive processing therapy are two well-studied options.

Why can accommodating symptoms backfire?

Accommodation means changing your own behavior to reduce your partner's distress. Examples include avoiding places that upset them, handling every errand in public, keeping quiet about your own feelings, or never raising hard subjects.

It comes from love, and in a crisis it can be the right call. Over time, though, the research is consistent. In a 2014 study of 46 treatment-seeking couples, more accommodation was linked to more severe PTSD symptoms, more depression and anger in both partners, and lower relationship satisfaction for both.

A 2022 study of 272 military couples followed them four times over 18 months. Higher PTSD symptoms predicted more partner accommodation at the next check-in. That accommodation then predicted more depressive symptoms in the partner. Across couples, more accommodation also tracked with lower partner relationship satisfaction.

The reason is simple. Avoidance keeps PTSD going, and accommodation can quietly extend it. The VA's clinician guidance notes that accommodation may also undermine treatment.

Pulling back from accommodation is best done gradually and together, ideally with a therapist. Suddenly forcing someone into a feared situation is not the answer.

Is there couples therapy for PTSD that works?

Yes. Cognitive-Behavioral Conjoint Therapy for PTSD is a 15-session therapy for the person with PTSD and their partner. It aims to reduce PTSD symptoms and improve the relationship at the same time. It moves through education about PTSD, communication skills and reducing avoidance, and then work on trauma-related beliefs.

In a randomized trial published in JAMA in 2012, 40 couples (heterosexual and same-sex) received CBCT right away or joined a wait list. CBCT led to significantly greater drops in clinician-rated PTSD severity. Patient relationship satisfaction also improved more, and gains held at 3-month follow-up.

A 2024 trial in service members and veterans compared CBCT with prolonged exposure, a first-line individual therapy, in 32 couples. PTSD symptoms improved in both groups. CBCT generally improved the relationship, while prolonged exposure did not do so consistently. Dropout was 27% for CBCT and 65% for prolonged exposure.

Both trials were small. The VA itself describes family-inclusive treatments as having preliminary support that needs more research. The VA also lists Structured Approach Therapy, a 12-session couples treatment designed for combat veterans and their partners.

If your partner prefers individual therapy, that is still an effective route. Couple work is an addition, not a requirement.

How do you take care of yourself as the partner?

The VA describes "caregiver burden," the sense that you must take on extra tasks, which can lead to stress, frustration, and fatigue. Partners report less happiness and lower quality of life. The accommodation research above links it to partner depression.

The VA recommends that partners:

  • Keep up exercise, sleep, and nutrition.
  • Protect friendships and hobbies outside the relationship.
  • Take time alone to recharge.
  • Get professional help if you feel depressed. Our sister site depression.md covers signs and treatment.

Free resources exist. The VA's PTSD Family Coach app offers education, stress tools, and support planning for family members. It is free on iOS and Android. If your partner is a veteran, the VA Caregiver Support Line at 1-855-260-3274 offers information, referrals, and short-term counseling for family members.

What should you do if anger, violence, or suicide risk shows up?

Most people with PTSD have never been violent, the VA notes. Still, there is a higher risk of partner conflict or violence when one or both partners have PTSD. For irritability that is hard to manage, see our article on PTSD and anger.

The VA suggests agreeing ahead of time that either of you can call a time-out, and the discussion stops right then. If anger becomes violence or abuse, go to a safe place and get help right away. The National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233.

If your partner talks about suicide or you fear for their safety, use the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. You can call or text 988, or chat online, 24/7. It is free and confidential, and you can reach out on behalf of someone else. Veterans, service members, and their loved ones can call 988 and press 1 for the Veterans Crisis Line, or text 838255.

The bottom line

Supporting a partner with PTSD works best when you understand the symptoms, listen more than you advise, and back effective treatment. Avoid building your life around the symptoms, because research links long-term accommodation to more distress for both partners. CBCT offers an evidence-based way to treat PTSD and the relationship together. Protect your own health, and use 988 without hesitation in a crisis.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I ask my partner to tell me what happened during the trauma?

No. Let your partner decide what and how much to share. The VA advises telling your loved one you want to listen, without pushing for details. Trauma-focused therapy is the right place to work through the memory itself.

Can I go to my partner's PTSD therapy sessions?

Sometimes. The VA suggests offering to go to doctor visits, and some therapies, such as CBCT for PTSD, are designed for both partners. For individual therapy, ask your partner and their clinician whether a joint session would help.

Is it my fault that my partner's PTSD symptoms get worse around me?

No. Symptoms such as irritability, withdrawal, and numbness come from PTSD, not from your failings. Stress and reminders can make them flare. A therapist can help you both sort out what is a symptom and what is a relationship issue.

What if my partner refuses to get treatment for PTSD?

The VA notes that withdrawal and reluctance can be symptoms of PTSD. Keep expressing care, share information, and avoid ultimatums unless your safety is at risk. Get support for yourself in the meantime, for example through your own counselor or, for veteran families, the VA Caregiver Support Line at 1-855-260-3274.

Is the PTSD Family Coach app free?

Yes. The VA National Center for PTSD made the PTSD Family Coach app, and it is free on iOS and Android. It covers PTSD education, self-care, and managing the relationship. It does not replace professional care.
