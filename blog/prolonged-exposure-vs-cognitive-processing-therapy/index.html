Prolonged Exposure vs Cognitive Processing Therapy: Which Works Better?

The short answer: Both work, and the gap between them is small. In the largest head-to-head trial, 916 veterans got either prolonged exposure (PE) or cognitive processing therapy (CPT). PE reduced PTSD symptoms slightly more, and more PE patients lost the PTSD diagnosis (40.4% vs 28.2% right after treatment). But the difference did not reach the trial's bar for clinical significance, and PE had more dropout. Every major guideline recommends both. Pick the one you can finish.

What is prolonged exposure (PE)?

PE is a trauma-focused therapy built on the idea that avoidance keeps PTSD alive. Edna Foa developed it from emotional processing theory. The VA National Center for PTSD describes the standard course as 8 to 15 sessions of 90 minutes, usually once a week.

PE has two core exercises. Imaginal exposure means telling the story of the trauma out loud, in detail, then listening to a recording of it between sessions. The VA patient guide says this usually starts around session 3. In vivo exposure means building a list of safe people, places, and activities you have been avoiding, then approaching them step by step.

The VA reports that about 53% of people who start PE no longer meet criteria for PTSD, and about 68% of those who complete it.

What is cognitive processing therapy (CPT)?

CPT targets the beliefs that trauma leaves behind, not the memory itself. Patricia Resick and colleagues designed it as a 12-session protocol. The VA patient guide lists individual sessions of 60 minutes, or 90-minute group sessions of 6 to 10 people, over about 3 months.

The key concept is the stuck point. The VA clinician page describes two kinds. Assimilation twists the facts to fit old beliefs ("It was my fault"). Over-accommodation swings too far the other way ("No one can be trusted"). You test these thoughts against evidence with worksheets.

CPT comes in two versions. The original includes a written account of the trauma that you read to yourself and your therapist. A cognitive-only version skips the account. The VA notes both versions produce similar outcomes, so patients can choose.

What do head-to-head trials show?

Two trials have compared the therapies directly. The first was Resick's 2002 study in the Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology. It randomized 171 women with rape-related PTSD to CPT, PE, or a waiting list. Both therapies were delivered twice weekly for 13 hours total, and both beat the waiting list by a wide margin. After treatment, 53% of the CPT group and 53% of the PE group no longer met PTSD criteria (counting everyone randomized). Dropout was almost identical: 26.8% for CPT and 27.3% for PE. The one difference: CPT did better on two of four trauma-related guilt scales.

The second and much larger trial was Schnurr and colleagues, JAMA Network Open, 2022. It randomized 916 veterans at VA medical centers to PE (455) or CPT (461), using a flexible protocol of 10 to 14 sessions. Key results:

  • Both groups improved a lot on the CAPS-5, the clinician interview for PTSD.
  • PE beat CPT by 2.42 points on the CAPS-5 (an 80-point scale). That was statistically significant (P = .01) but below the trial's threshold for a clinically meaningful difference.
  • Loss of PTSD diagnosis was 40.4% for PE and 28.2% for CPT after treatment, and 37.6% vs 28.9% at 6 months.
  • Full remission was 20.4% for PE and 12.6% for CPT.
  • Dropout was 55.8% for PE and 46.6% for CPT.

The authors' conclusion was direct: PE was statistically more effective, but the gap was not clinically significant, and both treatments produced meaningful improvement. They recommended shared decision-making rather than steering everyone to one therapy. The VA's own summary of the evidence calls PE and CPT "equally effective" in head-to-head comparisons.

What do the VA/DoD and APA guidelines recommend?

Both guidelines put PE and CPT in the top tier. The 2023 VA/DoD clinical practice guideline names three trauma-focused therapies with its strongest endorsement: CPT, EMDR, and PE. An ISTSS summary of that guideline states that "Psychotherapy is recommended over pharmacotherapy for treatment of PTSD based on larger and more persistent improvement with the former."

The APA's 2017 guideline reached the same place. A comparison of five PTSD guidelines by Hamblen and colleagues, hosted by the VA, shows APA gave strong recommendations to cognitive behavioral therapy, PE, CPT, and cognitive therapy. APA rated EMDR, brief eclectic psychotherapy, and narrative exposure therapy one level lower. If you want to compare a third option, see our guide to what to expect from EMDR.

Which therapy do people stick with?

This is where the two differ most. In the 2022 veterans trial, 55.8% of PE patients and 46.6% of CPT patients dropped out. A secondary analysis of that trial found the risk of quitting PE was highest right after session 3, which is when imaginal exposure begins. Longer gaps between sessions raised dropout risk in both arms by about 7% per extra day.

Across many trials the picture is mixed. The same analysis cites a 2021 meta-analysis that put dropout at roughly 34% for CPT and 28% for PE, with higher rates in routine care. In Resick's 2002 civilian trial the rates were equal. So PE is not always the harder therapy to finish. Veterans, younger patients, and people with heavy depression drop out more from both. Which therapy fits which person?

The trials cannot pick for you, but the mechanisms point in useful directions.

  • Avoidance-heavy PTSD. If you steer around places, people, crowds, or driving, PE targets that directly through in vivo exposure. The VA calls avoidance the core problem PE is built to solve.
  • Guilt and shame. The VA clinician guide lists self-blame, shame, hindsight bias, and guilt as presentations CPT handles well. Resick's 2002 trial found CPT beat PE on two guilt measures. NIMH describes cognitive restructuring as helpful when people "feel guilt or shame about something that is not their fault."
  • Not wanting to retell the story. Cognitive-only CPT never requires a detailed spoken or written account. PE requires it.
  • Preference. In the veterans trial, getting your preferred treatment did not predict staying in treatment, but satisfaction was high and equal for both. The VA's PTSD Treatment Decision Aid lets you compare therapies and watch videos before you meet a provider.

PE belongs to the same family as exposure and response prevention, the first-line therapy for OCD. If intrusive thoughts and rituals are part of your picture, ocd.md covers that approach. Veterans facing combat trauma or military sexual trauma can read more on PTSD in veterans.

How do you find a trained therapist?

Training matters more than the label. The VA's therapist-finder page points to the CPT Provider Roster, the PE directories run by Emory University and the University of Pennsylvania, and the ISTSS clinician directory. It also lists the APA psychologist locator and SAMHSA's Behavioral Health Services Locator for general searches.

Ask any candidate two questions. Do you have experience treating trauma? Do you deliver CPT or PE by the manual? Veterans can get either therapy at almost all VA medical centers, with video sessions at smaller clinics. The VA PTSD Program Locator lists the specialized programs.

The bottom line

PE and CPT are the two best-studied talk therapies for PTSD, and they finish close to even. In 916 veterans, PE won by 2.42 CAPS-5 points, and 40.4% of PE patients lost the diagnosis versus 28.2% on CPT. That edge was real but small, and PE lost more patients along the way. In civilian rape survivors the two were equal. The VA/DoD and APA both give the two therapies their strongest recommendation. Choose based on what you can commit to: facing the memory and the avoided situations (PE), or rewriting the beliefs the trauma left behind (CPT). The therapy you complete is the one that works better.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is PE or CPT faster?

They take about the same time. CPT is a 12-session protocol, and PE runs 8 to 15 sessions. Both usually finish in about 3 months. PE sessions are longer (up to 90 minutes) and include daily homework of listening to recordings and doing in vivo exposures.

Do I have to describe my trauma in detail in CPT?

Not necessarily. The original CPT includes a written account that you read to your therapist. A cognitive-only version leaves the account out, and the VA reports both versions produce similar results. PE always includes imaginal exposure, which means retelling the memory out loud.

Can I switch from one therapy to the other?

Yes. The VA/DoD guideline recommends any of the three trauma-focused therapies (PE, CPT, or EMDR), and a person who does not respond to one can try another. Tell your therapist early if a component feels unworkable, because most dropout happens in the first few sessions.

Are PE and CPT better than medication?

For most people, yes. The 2023 VA/DoD guideline recommends trauma-focused psychotherapy over medication because the improvement is larger and lasts longer. Sertraline, paroxetine, and venlafaxine remain options when therapy is not available or not wanted, and some people use both.

Does PE make PTSD worse before it gets better?

Distress often rises when imaginal exposure starts around session 3, and that is when dropout risk peaks. Symptoms then fall for most people who continue. In the 916-veteran trial, serious adverse events led to dropout in only 3 people per treatment arm.
