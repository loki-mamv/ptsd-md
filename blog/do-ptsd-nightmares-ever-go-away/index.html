Do PTSD Nightmares Ever Go Away?

August 20, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: yes, but rarely on their own. Trauma nightmares can persist for decades without treatment, and waiting them out is not a strategy the evidence supports. They do respond to treatment. Imagery rehearsal therapy has the strongest research behind it. Trauma-focused psychotherapy for the PTSD itself often reduces nightmares as a byproduct. Medication is the weakest leg of the stool, and the largest trial of the most commonly prescribed drug was negative.

That last point matters, because prazosin is still what most people hear about first.

How common are nightmares in PTSD?

Very. In the National Vietnam Veterans Readjustment Study, 52% of combat veterans with PTSD reported significant nightmares. In a general community sample, 71% of people with PTSD endorsed them (VA National Center for PTSD).

What makes trauma nightmares distinct from ordinary bad dreams is the replay quality. They often reproduce elements of the actual event, they recur, and they wake the person in a state of physiological alarm rather than simple unease. They also tend to be sticky. The VA notes that trauma-related nightmares are reported by many people whose traumatic event occurred as long as 40 years earlier.

Why do they persist for so long?

Two reinforcing loops keep them going.

The first is avoidance. Nightmares make sleep frightening, so people delay bedtime, drink to fall asleep, or sleep in a chair with the lights on. That produces sleep deprivation, which increases REM pressure and rebound, which increases nightmares.

The second is hyperarousal. PTSD keeps the threat-detection system running at elevated gain, including during sleep. Fragmented, shallow, arousal-heavy sleep is worse at doing the emotional processing that normally strips the fear charge off a memory over time. So the memory never gets filed. Our guide to the four PTSD symptom clusters covers where nightmares sit in the diagnostic picture.

What is imagery rehearsal therapy, and does it work?

Imagery rehearsal therapy (IRT) is a short, structured cognitive technique aimed at the nightmare itself rather than at the trauma. You write out the nightmare, then deliberately change it, usually the ending or a key detail, into something you choose. Then you rehearse the new version mentally for 10 to 20 minutes a day while awake.

It sounds too simple to matter. The evidence says otherwise. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2018 position paper on nightmare disorder assessed more than 100 studies and found nine randomized controlled trials enrolling close to 800 participants showing IRT reduced nightmare frequency and distress, in nightmares associated with PTSD and in nightmares with no known cause. The AASM recommended it for the treatment of nightmare disorder in adults (Morgenthaler et al., Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine 2018).

Courses are typically brief, often four to eight sessions. It is not exposure therapy and it does not require narrating the trauma in detail, which is part of why some people accept it when they are not ready for trauma-focused work.

What about prazosin?

Prazosin is an alpha-1 adrenergic blocker, originally a blood pressure drug, repurposed on the theory that blunting nighttime noradrenaline activity should quiet trauma nightmares. For roughly fifteen years it was the standard pharmacological answer, supported by several positive randomized trials.

Then came the definitive test. A trial published in the New England Journal of Medicine in February 2018, supported by the VA Cooperative Studies Program, enrolled veterans with chronic PTSD and frequent nightmares across 13 VA medical centers. Participants received prazosin or placebo for 26 weeks, escalated over five weeks to a maximum of 20 mg daily in men and 12 mg daily in women. Prazosin did not separate from placebo on nightmares or on PTSD symptoms (Raskind et al., NEJM 2018).

The 2023 VA/DoD Clinical Practice Guideline for PTSD landed on a split verdict. It offers a weak recommendation for prazosin specifically for nightmares, and it suggests against prazosin for global PTSD symptoms (VA/DoD 2023 guideline).

How to read all that in plain terms: prazosin helps some people, it is not reliable, and it should not be the only thing you try. It also lowers blood pressure and can cause dizziness on standing, so it needs a real prescriber conversation rather than a request for a refill.

Does treating the PTSD fix the nightmares?

Often, and this is the most underused answer. Nightmares are an intrusion symptom. When overall PTSD severity comes down, intrusion symptoms usually come down with it.

The 2023 VA/DoD guideline gives its strongest recommendations to trauma-focused psychotherapies: prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, and eye movement desensitization and reprocessing. Our explainer on how EMDR works walks through what those sessions look like. If the trauma was prolonged or repeated rather than a single event, our guide to complex PTSD covers how treatment differs.

The practical sequencing question, if nightmares are the loudest symptom, is whether to treat them first or treat the PTSD first. Many clinicians run IRT alongside trauma-focused therapy rather than choosing, because restoring some sleep makes the harder work more tolerable.

What else should be ruled out?

Three things routinely make trauma nightmares worse and are easy to miss:

  • Obstructive sleep apnea. It is common in people with PTSD, and it fragments sleep and drives arousals out of REM, the stage where most nightmares occur. If you snore, wake gasping, or feel unrefreshed after a full night, ask about a sleep study.
  • Alcohol. It shortens the time to fall asleep and then suppresses REM early in the night, producing REM rebound in the second half. That rebound is a well-documented nightmare amplifier, which is why drinking to sleep so often backfires by 3 a.m.
  • Untreated insomnia. Insomnia and nightmares travel together and worsen each other. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia is a separate, well-validated treatment, covered in more detail at insomnia.md.

What a reasonable plan looks like

If nightmares are the presenting problem, a sensible sequence looks roughly like this:

  1. Screen for sleep apnea and review alcohol and medications with a clinician.
  2. Start a nightmare-specific treatment with evidence behind it, usually IRT, and treat insomnia if it is present.
  3. Get into a trauma-focused psychotherapy for the PTSD itself, since that is where the strongest recommendations sit.
  4. Consider medication as an addition rather than a substitute, understanding that the evidence for prazosin is contested.

Veterans can access all of this through VA care, and our guide to PTSD in veterans covers how to start that process.

The bottom line

PTSD nightmares do not reliably fade with time. They can persist for decades, and a large share of people with PTSD still report them long after the event. But they are treatable, and the treatment with the best evidence is a brief behavioral technique rather than a drug. Imagery rehearsal therapy is recommended by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine on the strength of nine randomized trials. Trauma-focused psychotherapy reduces nightmares by reducing the disorder that produces them. Prazosin may help some people and failed its largest test.

If you are waiting for the nightmares to stop on their own, the evidence says stop waiting and start treating.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do PTSD nightmares go away on their own?

Often they do not. The VA National Center for PTSD notes that trauma-related nightmares are reported by a large share of people with PTSD even when the traumatic event happened decades earlier. Time alone is not a reliable treatment. Nightmares do respond to targeted treatment, which is a different and more useful fact.

What is the most effective treatment for PTSD nightmares?

Imagery rehearsal therapy (IRT) has the strongest evidence. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2018 position paper reviewed nine randomized controlled trials enrolling close to 800 participants and recommended IRT for nightmare disorder, including nightmares associated with PTSD. IRT involves rewriting the nightmare's storyline while awake and mentally rehearsing the new version for 10 to 20 minutes a day.

Does prazosin work for PTSD nightmares?

The evidence is genuinely mixed. Several earlier randomized trials found prazosin reduced trauma nightmares, but the largest and most rigorous trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2018 and run across 13 VA medical centers over 26 weeks, found no significant benefit over placebo. The 2023 VA/DoD clinical practice guideline still offers a weak recommendation for prazosin specifically for nightmares, while suggesting against it for overall PTSD symptoms.

Will treating my PTSD stop the nightmares?

Frequently, yes. Nightmares are a symptom of the intrusion cluster of PTSD, and trauma-focused psychotherapies such as prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, and EMDR are the strongest-recommended PTSD treatments in the 2023 VA/DoD guideline. As overall PTSD severity falls, nightmares usually fall with it. When they do not, a nightmare-specific treatment such as IRT can be added.

Can sleep apnea make PTSD nightmares worse?

It can contribute. Obstructive sleep apnea is common in people with PTSD, and it fragments sleep and increases arousals from REM sleep, the stage where most nightmares occur. If you snore, wake gasping, or feel unrefreshed despite adequate time in bed, ask about a sleep study. Treating the apnea does not treat the PTSD, but leaving it untreated makes every sleep symptom harder to fix.
