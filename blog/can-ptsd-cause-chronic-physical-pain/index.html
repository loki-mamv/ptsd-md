Can PTSD Cause Chronic Physical Pain?

October 12, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida
Table of Contents

The short answer: PTSD and chronic pain often occur together, and each can make the other worse. Research shows a consistent link, but it does not prove that PTSD alone causes pain. In one meta-analysis, 9.7% of people with chronic pain had PTSD, and 20.5% of those with chronic widespread pain did. Trauma-focused therapy may ease both problems.

How often do PTSD and chronic pain occur together?

Chronic pain is common on its own. The VA National Center for PTSD says it affects about 1 in 5 U.S. adults. The same page says chronic pain is common in people with PTSD, and that veterans have more chronic pain than people who did not serve.

Most of the hard numbers come from studies of people who already have chronic pain. They measure how many of those people also have PTSD.

  • Pooled estimate: A 2017 meta-analysis of 21 studies found PTSD in 9.7% of people with chronic pain. Single studies ranged from 0% to 57%.
  • Pain clinics versus the community: The same analysis found 11.7% in clinical groups and 5.1% in non-clinical groups.
  • Veterans: A 2017 systematic review of 40 studies reported PTSD in 50.1% of veterans with chronic pain and in 9.8% of the general population with chronic pain.

The method matters. Studies that used self-report questionnaires found PTSD in 20.4% of people with chronic pain. Studies that used structured clinical interviews found 4.5%. Treat the questionnaire figures with care.

The systematic review also asked a simpler question. Of 19 studies that tested for a link between chronic pain and PTSD, 16 found one (84.2%).

Why would PTSD make pain worse or longer lasting?

The leading explanation is the mutual maintenance model. A 2001 review by Sharp and Harvey concluded that chronic pain and PTSD keep each other going through several pathways. The model describes a loop. It does not say one condition simply causes the other.

The VA describes how that loop can work in daily life:

  • Pain as a reminder: If one event caused both the injury and the PTSD, the pain can bring the trauma back to mind.
  • Body scanning: PTSD keeps you alert for danger. That same alertness makes it easier to notice pain and a racing heartbeat. Our guide to hypervigilance in PTSD explains this state.
  • Poor sleep: Nightmares and pain both break up sleep. Lack of sleep leaves you less able to cope with either. Our sister site insomnia.md covers sleep problems in depth.
  • Avoidance: Avoiding trauma reminders and avoiding painful movement both make you less active and less social. Lost fitness can then increase pain.
  • Loss of control: Both conditions can leave people feeling that their body and life are out of their hands.

Shared causes matter too. Serious accidents, assault, disaster and combat can cause PTSD and a physical injury at the same time. The VA notes that not every such injury leads to chronic pain.

Symptom severity tracks with pain. In baseline data from a pain trial of 421 men and 386 women veterans, higher PTSD symptoms were linked to more pain intensity, more pain interference and more catastrophizing. The pattern was the same in both sexes. This was a snapshot at one point in time, so it cannot show which problem came first.

Which kinds of pain are most linked to PTSD?

The link is strongest for widespread pain and weakest for back pain. The 2017 meta-analysis reported these PTSD rates by pain type:

  • Chronic widespread pain (the pattern seen in fibromyalgia): 20.5%
  • Headache: 11.2%
  • Back pain: 0.3%

The systematic review found a similar low figure for chronic low back pain: 0.69%.

A 2014 meta-analysis of 71 studies looked at the question from the other side. People who reported trauma were 2.7 times more likely to have a functional somatic syndrome. That group includes fibromyalgia, chronic widespread pain, temporomandibular disorder and irritable bowel syndrome. The association was larger for PTSD than for a history of physical or sexual abuse.

The authors rated most of the underlying studies as low quality. These figures show association, and they come from comparison studies that cannot prove cause.

Does treating PTSD help the pain?

It may, and trauma-focused therapy has the best evidence so far. A 2026 meta-analysis in the journal Pain pooled 30 randomized trials with 3,245 participants. Each trial measured both pain intensity and PTSD symptoms.

  • Trauma-focused treatments gave a medium improvement in PTSD symptoms and a small improvement in pain intensity.
  • Cognitive-behavioral therapies, mind-body therapies and peripheral modulation showed no significant effect on either outcome.
  • The evidence was rated low quality, and most studies were methodologically weak.

The authors concluded that treatment for both conditions should, at minimum, include a trauma-focused component. Prolonged exposure and cognitive processing therapy are two such treatments. See our comparison of prolonged exposure and cognitive processing therapy.

Integrated programs treat both conditions in one plan. VA researchers built a 12-session integrated treatment that joins cognitive processing therapy with pain-focused cognitive behavioral therapy. Its pilot enrolled six veterans, and three finished. Results suggested the approach was feasible.

Not every program works. A 2024 randomized trial tested a self-directed app and web program for veterans with both conditions and their partners. It found no significant change in overall pain or PTSD symptoms. It did reduce how much pain interfered with mood and sleep.

The VA says care can treat one condition at a time or both together. What matters most is that every provider knows about both problems so that care is coordinated.

Which medicines need extra care when you have both?

Three points are worth raising with a prescriber.

Opioids. The CDC 2022 opioid prescribing guideline states that nonopioid therapies are preferred for subacute and chronic pain. The VA adds that opioids do not cure pain and tend to work less well over time. PTSD may raise the risk of long-term use. In a meta-analysis of 15 studies and 416,321 joint replacement patients, PTSD was linked to a 34% higher risk of prolonged opioid use after surgery.

Benzodiazepines. The VA clinician guide to PTSD medications says studies have not shown that benzodiazepines help PTSD. It lists concerns that include addiction, falls and interference with therapy. The CDC guideline also urges particular caution when benzodiazepines are prescribed with opioids.

Antidepressants that also treat pain. Only sertraline and paroxetine are FDA-approved for PTSD, according to the VA guide. The FDA label for duloxetine lists fibromyalgia, diabetic nerve pain and chronic musculoskeletal pain among its approved uses, but not PTSD. The 2023 VA/DoD guideline found insufficient evidence to recommend for or against duloxetine for PTSD. It does recommend venlafaxine, a related medicine. Our guide to FDA-approved medications for PTSD has more detail.

Do not stop or change a prescribed medicine on your own.

When should you see a doctor about pain?

Get new, severe or changing pain checked first. PTSD does not rule out an injury, arthritis, nerve damage or another medical cause. A link to PTSD is something to consider after a medical evaluation, not a replacement for one.

When you see a clinician, it helps to say these things plainly:

  • You have both pain and trauma symptoms, even if they seem unrelated.
  • Whether the pain started with the traumatic event.
  • Whether pain flares follow bad nights, reminders or high-stress days.
  • Every medicine you take, including those from other prescribers.

The VA also advises asking a doctor whether exercise is safe for you. Once it is, a gradual increase in movement and enjoyable activity can help break the cycle of pain and inactivity.

If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, call 988 in the United States. Veterans can press 1 after calling.

The bottom line

PTSD and chronic pain are closely linked, most of all in veterans and in people with widespread pain. The best explanation is a two-way loop: pain feeds trauma symptoms, and trauma symptoms feed pain. Research has not shown that PTSD by itself creates pain in a healthy body.

Trauma-focused therapy may improve both conditions, although the evidence is still low quality. Tell every provider about both problems, and ask how your pain plan and your PTSD plan fit together.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the pain from PTSD real or is it in my head?

The pain is real. Research links PTSD symptoms to higher pain intensity and more interference with daily life. The link does not mean the pain is imagined, and it does not rule out a physical cause that needs its own evaluation.

Is fibromyalgia linked to PTSD?

Yes, studies show an association. A 2017 meta-analysis found PTSD in 20.5% of people with chronic widespread pain, the pattern seen in fibromyalgia. That is about twice the 9.7% rate across all chronic pain. These studies show a link and do not prove that PTSD causes fibromyalgia.

Will my pain go away if my PTSD is treated?

It may improve, but no study promises that it will go away. A 2026 meta-analysis of 30 trials found that trauma-focused treatments gave a small improvement in pain intensity and a medium improvement in PTSD symptoms. The evidence was rated low quality, so results for one person can differ.

Are opioids a good choice for chronic pain when you have PTSD?

The CDC 2022 guideline says nonopioid therapies are preferred for subacute and chronic pain in general. The VA notes that opioids do not cure pain and tend to work less well over time. One meta-analysis linked PTSD to a higher risk of prolonged opioid use after joint replacement. Decide with your prescriber.

Should I treat the pain or the PTSD first?

Either order can work. The VA says care can address one condition at a time or both together. The key step is to make sure all of your providers know about both, so they can coordinate your care.
