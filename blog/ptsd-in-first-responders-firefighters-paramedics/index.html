How Common Is PTSD in First Responders Like Firefighters and Paramedics?

October 10, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: About 1 in 10 to 1 in 7 active first responders screens positive for current PTSD in pooled studies. The largest review to date found 14.3% in responders with routine job exposures. The VA estimates about 5% for U.S. adults in a given year. Most first responders do not develop PTSD, and proven treatments exist for those who do.

How common is PTSD in first responders?

No single survey counts every firefighter, paramedic and police officer. The best figures come from meta-analyses, which pool many smaller studies. Each figure below is a point estimate: the share with PTSD at the time of the study.

  • Rescue workers, 2012: Berger and colleagues pooled 28 studies with 20,424 rescuers worldwide. Current PTSD prevalence was 10%.
  • Rescue workers, 2024: A meta-regression in Stress and Health found 9.8%. The authors called this virtually the same as the figure from 10 years before.
  • Active first responders, 2025: A review of 138 studies published after 2008 found 14.3% in responders with routine exposures. It found 8.3% in samples studied after a large-scale disaster.

For comparison, the VA National Center for PTSD estimates that about 5 of every 100 U.S. adults have PTSD in any given year. About 6 of every 100 have it at some point in life. The pooled first responder figures are worldwide, so the comparison is approximate.

One number needs a caution. A 2018 SAMHSA research bulletin cites an estimate that 30% of first responders develop behavioral health conditions. That figure covers depression, PTSD and other conditions together. It is not a PTSD rate, although many articles quote it as one.

Do firefighters, paramedics and police have different rates?

The evidence is mixed, and the newest data suggest the differences are small.

  • Paramedics and ambulance staff: A 2018 meta-analysis reviewed 27 studies covering 30,878 ambulance personnel. Estimated prevalence was 11% for PTSD, 15% for depression and 15% for anxiety.
  • Police: A 2020 meta-analysis covered 272,463 police personnel in 24 countries. Pooled PTSD prevalence was 14.2% (95% confidence interval 10.3% to 18.7%).
  • Firefighters: The global reviews cited here include firefighters in the pooled totals. Their summaries do not report one worldwide firefighter figure.

The 2012 review found higher rates in ambulance personnel than in firefighters and police. The 2025 review found no difference between occupations. Its authors concluded that all first responder occupations appear equally prone to PTSD.

Leaving the job does not end the risk. A 2026 meta-analysis of former first responders found a pooled prevalence of 15.4% across eight studies.

Why do the estimates vary so much?

Single studies report figures far below and far above the pooled numbers. The reviews name these reasons:

  • How PTSD was measured. The 2024 and 2025 reviews both found that prevalence changed with the assessment tool. Many studies use a self-report questionnaire with a cutoff score. Researchers often call that result "probable PTSD". It is a screening result, not a diagnosis by a clinician.
  • Where the study was done. The 2012 review found higher rates in Asian studies than in European ones. The 2025 review found higher rates in low and middle-income countries than in high-income countries.
  • Routine work or one disaster. Responders with routine exposures had a higher rate (14.3%) than those studied after one large disaster (8.3%).
  • Paid or volunteer. Non-volunteers had higher prevalence than volunteers in the 2025 review.
  • When the data were collected. The 2025 review found some evidence of a rise over time, mostly since the COVID-19 pandemic.

A workplace survey can also miss people who are on sick leave or who left the job because of symptoms. Read any single percentage with its population and its method.

What raises the risk of PTSD in first responders?

The work itself is the main exposure. Combat veterans face a similar pattern of repeated trauma, which we cover in PTSD in veterans. The SAMHSA bulletin and the police meta-analysis report these risk factors:

  • Being seriously injured or harmed on the job
  • Handling serious injuries or the bodies of the dead
  • Longer time at a disaster scene, and early arrival
  • Identifying closely with survivors
  • High occupational stress and avoidant coping
  • Poor relationships with coworkers and dissatisfaction with supervisors
  • Personal trauma or loss before the event

Support protects. In the police meta-analysis, higher peer support was linked to significantly lower PTSD symptoms. The SAMHSA bulletin lists specialized training, longer time in the job and approachable leaders as protective factors.

PTSD often occurs with other problems. The police meta-analysis found depression in 14.6% and hazardous drinking in 25.7%. Shift work and night calls also disrupt sleep, and our sibling site insomnia.md covers sleep problems in detail. Some responders also carry guilt about calls that went wrong, which may fit moral injury more than PTSD.

Why do many first responders not get help?

Stigma is the largest documented barrier. A 2017 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 12 samples of first responders. It found that 33.1% endorsed stigma about mental health care. The most common fears were about confidentiality and harm to their career.

Practical barriers were less common. In the same review, 9.3% endorsed barriers to care. The most frequent were scheduling problems and not knowing where to get help. The authors warned that stigma can delay care, which may let symptoms become chronic.

Retirement adds a problem. The 2026 review of former first responders noted that they lose the services and social support their old job gave them.

What treatment works for first responders with PTSD?

The same treatments that work for other adults. The VA National Center for PTSD says the talk therapies that work best are trauma-focused:

  • Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT)
  • Prolonged Exposure (PE)
  • Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR)

The VA says these therapies usually last about 8 to 16 sessions. It names paroxetine, sertraline and venlafaxine as the medications with the most evidence. A prescriber decides if a medication fits. Our comparison of prolonged exposure and CPT explains how the two therapies differ.

Research in first responders supports this, with limits. A 2022 meta-analysis of 15 controlled studies found that psychological interventions significantly reduced PTSD symptoms in first responders. CBT-based and clinician-delivered treatments did better than other types. The authors rated the risk of bias as moderate to high in all studies.

A 2022 systematic review of EMDR in first responders found eight studies. EMDR appeared to relieve work-related trauma symptoms. All eight studies were of weak or medium quality, and the results for early intervention were inconclusive.

The bottom line

Pooled studies put current PTSD at about 10% to 14% of active first responders. That is higher than the 5% the VA estimates for U.S. adults in a year. Rates look similar for firefighters, paramedics and police in the newest data. Any single figure depends on the country, the measurement tool and the type of exposure. Stigma keeps many responders from care, but trauma-focused therapy works. If symptoms disrupt work, sleep or relationships, talk to a doctor or a licensed mental health professional.

Frequently Asked Questions

What percentage of firefighters have PTSD?

There is no single agreed figure. Pooled studies of rescue workers, which include firefighters, report current PTSD in about 10% to 14%. A 2025 meta-analysis of 138 studies found no significant difference between first responder occupations.

Are paramedics more likely to get PTSD than police officers?

Older data suggested so. A 2012 meta-analysis found higher rates in ambulance personnel than in firefighters and police. A larger 2025 meta-analysis found no difference between occupations, so the gap is probably small.

Is the claim that 30% of first responders have PTSD correct?

No. The 30% figure comes from an estimate cited in a 2018 SAMHSA bulletin. It covers all behavioral health conditions, including depression and PTSD together. Pooled estimates for PTSD alone are about 10% to 14%.

Does PTSD risk end when a first responder retires?

No. A 2026 meta-analysis of former first responders found a pooled PTSD prevalence of 15.4% across eight studies. The authors noted that former responders lose the services and social support their job gave them.

Who can a first responder call in a mental health crisis?

Call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. It is free, confidential and open 24 hours a day. Call 911 if someone is in immediate danger.
