Can PTSD Go Away On Its Own Without Treatment?

September 19, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Sometimes. Across 42 untreated cohorts, 44.0% of people with PTSD at baseline no longer met criteria after an average of 40 months (range 8% to 89%). In the WHO World Mental Health surveys, half of cases recovered within 24 months, but 23% had not recovered after 10 years. Watching and waiting is reasonable in the first month after a trauma, not after months of symptoms.

How often does PTSD remit without treatment?

The best estimate comes from a 2014 meta-analysis in Clinical Psychology Review by Morina and colleagues. It pooled 42 observational studies of PTSD without specific treatment, covering 81,642 participants. The mean observation period was 40 months. Across all studies, 44.0% of people who had PTSD at baseline were non-cases at follow-up.

That average hides enormous variation. Remission in individual studies ran from 8% to 89%. Two patterns stood out:

  • When the baseline assessment fell within five months of the trauma, 51.7% remitted. When baseline came later than five months, only 36.9% did.
  • PTSD after a natural disaster had the highest mean remission rate (60.0%). PTSD tied to physical disease had the lowest (31.4%).

No other measured predictor in that analysis was linked to remission. So the honest summary is that roughly four in ten untreated people improve enough to lose the diagnosis over about three years. The other six in ten do not.

How long does recovery take when it happens on its own?

The WHO World Mental Health surveys examined 1,575 respondents with lifetime PTSD. Recovery was slow and steady rather than quick:

  • 20% recovered within 3 months.
  • 27% recovered within 6 months.
  • 50% recovered within 24 months.
  • 77% recovered within 10 years.

The median time to recovery was therefore about two years. Nearly one in four people had still not recovered after a decade. The authors noted that recall bias affected recoveries reported after 24 months, so the later figures are soft.

A companion World Mental Health report measured symptom duration directly. Mean duration averaged 72.3 months, about six years, across all trauma types. Median duration was five years for war-related PTSD and three years after physical or intimate partner sexual violence. For other trauma categories the median ran one to two years.

Is early distress after a trauma the same as PTSD?

No, and that is the distinction most casual advice blurs. Most people exposed to trauma never develop PTSD. In the World Mental Health surveys, 70.4% of respondents reported a lifetime trauma, averaging 3.2 traumas per person. The conditional risk of PTSD after a randomly selected trauma was 4.0%.

The US figures line up. The National Center for PTSD estimates that about 6 of every 100 people will have PTSD at some point. About 5 of every 100 US adults have it in a given year. That was roughly 13 million Americans in 2020. Women are affected about twice as often as men (8% against 4%).

VA guidance is blunt about the early window: "Most people start to feel better after a few weeks." It also draws the line: "If symptoms last longer than a month and are causing problems in your life, it could be PTSD." Short-lived distress fading is not the same as diagnosed PTSD remitting. Our breakdown of PTSD symptoms covers what actually counts.

Who recovers without treatment, and who stays stuck?

Trauma type matters, though less than most people expect. Interpersonal violence carries the highest risk of developing PTSD in the first place. World Mental Health data put the conditional risk at 19.0% after rape, 11.7% after physical abuse by a romantic partner, 11.0% after being kidnapped, and 10.5% after other sexual assault. Direct exposure to death or serious injury carried a risk of 1.3%.

For recovery specifically, the WHO analysis found recovery was only weakly related to most trauma types. Very low early recovery (odds ratios of 0.2 to 0.3) was tied to purposely injuring, torturing or killing someone, and to witnessing atrocities. Very low later recovery was tied to being kidnapped. Pre-trauma factors predicted recovery poorly, which the authors attributed to stronger post-trauma influences.

Two older meta-analyses point the same way. Ozer and colleagues pooled 68 studies and found all seven tested predictors significant. Peritraumatic dissociation was strongest (weighted r = .35). Family history, prior trauma and prior adjustment were weakest (weighted r = .17). Brewin and colleagues reviewed 14 risk factors. They concluded that factors operating during or after the trauma, such as trauma severity, lack of social support and additional life stress, had somewhat stronger effects than pre-trauma factors.

Depression is a common travelling companion. The National Center for PTSD reports that depression is "nearly 3 to 5 times more likely in those with PTSD than those without PTSD." If low mood is the louder problem, depression.md covers that ground.

What do treatment guidelines say about waiting?

Both major guidelines allow a short wait, and both put hard limits on it.

NICE guideline NG116 recommendation 1.6.4 says: "Consider active monitoring for people with subthreshold symptoms of PTSD within 1 month of a traumatic event. Arrange follow-up contact to take place within 1 month." Recommendation 1.6.5 says: "Do not offer psychologically-focused debriefing for the prevention or treatment of PTSD."

The wait ends there. NICE 1.6.15 tells clinicians to offer individual trauma-focused CBT after trauma in the last month. That applies to adults with acute stress disorder or clinically important PTSD symptoms. NICE 1.6.16 says to offer the same to adults presenting more than one month after a traumatic event.

The 2023 VA/DoD clinical practice guideline lands in the same place. Recommendation 4 finds "insufficient evidence to recommend for or against psychotherapy or pharmacotherapy in the immediate post-trauma period" for preventing PTSD. Recommendation 5 states: "For the prevention of PTSD among patients diagnosed with acute stress disorder, we suggest trauma-focused cognitive behavioral psychotherapy."

Read together, the message is narrow. Active monitoring applies to mild, early, subthreshold symptoms. Once someone meets criteria, both guidelines point to treatment.

How much does treatment actually add?

A lot, and the comparison group in most trials is a waitlist, which is close to no treatment. A 2020 systematic review of 114 randomized trials covering 8,171 participants reported these post-treatment effect sizes against waitlist or usual care:

  • CBT with a trauma focus: standardized mean difference -1.32 (95% CI -1.57 to -1.08), from 51 trials and 1,380 participants. Graded moderate quality.
  • Prolonged exposure: -1.59 (95% CI -2.05 to -1.13), from 12 trials and 772 participants.
  • Cognitive processing therapy: -1.03 (95% CI -1.45 to -0.61), from 4 trials and 298 participants.
  • EMDR: -1.23 (95% CI -1.69 to -0.76), from 11 trials and 415 participants.

By convention, a standardized mean difference above 0.8 counts as large. Dropout is the catch. A companion review found a pooled dropout rate of 16% (95% CI 14% to 18%), and trauma-focused therapies had significantly greater dropout. NICE says trauma-focused CBT for adults should typically run 8 to 12 sessions, with more if clinically indicated. That is a short commitment set against a median untreated course of two years. Our guides to how long PTSD treatment takes and prolonged exposure versus cognitive processing therapy go deeper.

The bottom line

PTSD can remit without treatment, and for a minority it does so fairly quickly. For most people it does not, or it takes years. The 44.0% figure from untreated cohorts is not a reason to wait. It is a reminder that the majority did not recover.

A practical rule: give yourself the first month after a trauma, with sleep, safety, trusted people and a planned follow-up contact. If symptoms still disrupt your life after a month, that is the point where both NICE and the VA/DoD stop recommending watchful waiting. The National Center for PTSD puts it plainly: "For many people, treatment can get rid of symptoms altogether."

Frequently Asked Questions

If my PTSD symptoms improved on their own, can they come back?

Yes. The National Center for PTSD notes that symptoms may come and go over many years, and that they sometimes do not appear until months or years after the event. Anniversaries, new stressors and reminders can bring them back. A return of symptoms does not mean the earlier improvement was imaginary.

Does PTSD after a car accident clear faster than PTSD after an assault?

On average, yes. World Mental Health data show median symptom durations of one to two years for most trauma categories, three years after physical or intimate partner sexual violence, and five years after war-related trauma. Individual courses still vary a lot inside every category.

Is a waitlist in a trial the same thing as no treatment?

Not exactly, but it is the closest ethical approximation and it is the standard comparison arm. Waitlist participants get assessment and monitoring without active therapy. Trauma-focused therapies still beat waitlist and usual care by large margins in pooled trial data.

Does talking through the trauma right after it happens prevent PTSD?

Single-session psychological debriefing does not. NICE recommends against psychologically-focused debriefing for either prevention or treatment. The VA/DoD guideline cites blinded trials in which Critical Incident Stress Debriefing did not reduce PTSD incidence at six-month follow-up.

Why do studies disagree so much about untreated recovery?

The 2014 meta-analysis found remission between 8% and 89% across its 42 studies. Trauma type, how soon baseline was measured, and how remission was defined explain much of that spread. Studies with a baseline within five months of the trauma reported 51.7% remission against 36.9% for later baselines.
