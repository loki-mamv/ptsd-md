Do Service Dogs Actually Help With PTSD?

October 2, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Probably yes, but the effect is modest and the evidence is still thin. In a VA randomized trial of 227 veterans, service dogs cut PTSD symptom scores 3.7 points more than emotional support dogs did. Functioning and quality of life were no different between the two groups. A service dog adds to trauma-focused therapy. It does not replace it.

What does the research show?

Two controlled studies carry most of the weight. Both studied veterans or military members. Both found lower PTSD symptoms with a trained service dog. Neither found a cure.

The first is the VA trial, run through the VA Office of Research and Development and published in Psychiatric Services in 2023. The second is an NIH-funded study from the University of Arizona, published in JAMA Network Open in 2024. It compared 81 veterans who got a psychiatric service dog with 75 veterans still on the waiting list.

After 3 months, the Arizona study found these differences:

  • Self-reported PTSD symptoms: a mean PTSD Checklist score of 41.9 with a dog versus 51.7 without. The adjusted difference was 11.5 points on a scale of 0 to 80.
  • Clinician-rated PTSD symptoms: a mean score of 30.2 versus 36.9, a difference of 7.0 points. The raters did not know who had a dog.
  • Depression, anxiety, and social isolation: all lower in the service dog group.

One number keeps this in proportion. At 3 months, 75% of the service dog group still met the criteria for a PTSD diagnosis. The figure in the waiting-list group was 85%. Most veterans with a dog still had PTSD.

What did the VA study of service dogs versus emotional support dogs find?

This is the only large randomized trial of the two that carry the evidence. Researchers randomly assigned 227 veterans with PTSD to a service dog or an emotional support dog. Of those, 181 received a dog, and the team followed them for 18 months.

The published results were mixed:

  • Primary outcomes: no difference between the groups in overall functioning or quality of life.
  • PTSD symptoms: a 3.7-point greater reduction with a service dog (p=0.036).
  • Medication: antidepressant adherence rose 10 percentage points more in the service dog group.
  • Cost: no reduction in health care use or cost with a service dog.

The authors wrote that both groups appeared to benefit from having a dog. That statement needs care. The trial had no group without a dog, so it cannot show how much any dog helps. It shows only what task training adds on top of companionship. That added amount was small.

How strong is the evidence?

It is weaker than the evidence for therapy. The 2023 VA/DoD clinical practice guideline for PTSD states that there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against animal-assisted therapy, including canine therapy. The National Center for PTSD says there is not enough research to know if dogs help treat PTSD and its symptoms.

The study designs explain that caution:

  • The Arizona study was not randomized. Veterans got a dog based on their place on the waiting list. The two groups could differ in ways the study did not measure.
  • No one can be blinded to a dog. Veterans knew their group, and most outcomes were self-reported. Expectation can move those scores.
  • Follow-up was short. The Arizona study measured outcomes at 3 months only.
  • One organization trained all the dogs in the Arizona study. Results from other trainers may differ.
  • The participants wanted a dog. The authors say the findings may not apply to veterans who do not seek one.
  • The studies were in veterans. Neither trial tested service dogs in civilians with PTSD.

Only 25% of the Arizona participants were in evidence-based PTSD treatment when the study started. The trial therefore says little about how a dog works together with therapy.

What is the difference between a service dog and an emotional support animal?

The difference is training, and the law follows the training. ADA rules define a service animal as a dog that is individually trained to do work or perform tasks for a person with a disability. The ADA guidance gives calming a person with PTSD during an anxiety attack as one example of such a task.

A dog whose sole function is comfort or emotional support does not qualify as a service animal under the ADA. An emotional support dog is a pet, and it has no general right to enter public places where pets are not allowed.

The ADA also limits what a business may ask. Staff may ask if the dog is a service animal required because of a disability. They may ask what task the dog is trained to perform. They may not require medical documents, a special ID card, or proof of training.

For a person whose main problem is constant scanning for danger in public, task training is the relevant part. Our guide to hypervigilance in PTSD explains that symptom.

Does the VA provide or pay for service dogs for PTSD?

VA does not hand out dogs. The VA service dog benefit page states that VA does not provide service dogs but does provide veterinary health care and other benefits. The dog and veteran must complete training with an organization accredited by Assistance Dogs International or the International Guide Dog Federation.

For mental health conditions, the benefit goes through a VA mental health provider. The care team decides if the condition is the primary cause of substantial mobility limits and if a dog is the best approach. The benefit covers veterinary insurance, prescribed medications, and travel to get the dog. It does not cover food, grooming, or boarding.

The PAWS for Veterans Therapy Act became law on August 25, 2021. It did two things:

  • It ordered a 5-year pilot program at no fewer than five VA medical centers. Veterans with PTSD help train service dogs as a complementary health activity.
  • It gave VA the authority to provide a service dog to a veteran with a mental illness, regardless of mobility impairment. The law says VA "may" do this. It does not say VA must.

The pilot is not a route to a dog of your own. At VA Palo Alto, one of five sites, the program is 8 weekly sessions of about 1.5 hours. The site states that veterans will not receive a service animal after they complete it.

The primary sources above give no price for a PTSD service dog and no typical wait time. The Arizona study does confirm that a waiting list exists: its control group was on it. Ask an accredited organization for its current wait and fees. More VA context is in our guide to PTSD in veterans.

Can a service dog replace therapy or medication?

No. The National Center for PTSD states that dogs can help with some parts of living with PTSD but are not a substitute for effective PTSD treatment. Both major studies describe the dog as an addition to usual care.

The center also gives a warning. If a person relies on the dog to avoid feared situations, that can get in the way of recovery. Trauma-focused therapy works by helping a person face those situations and memories. A dog that makes avoidance easier can work against that goal.

The VA/DoD guideline recommends trauma-focused psychotherapies first. Our comparison of prolonged exposure and cognitive processing therapy covers two of them. If anxiety outside of PTSD is part of the picture, anxiety.md covers those conditions.

The bottom line

Service dogs are linked to lower PTSD symptoms in veterans, and the best randomized trial found a small added benefit over an emotional support dog. The same trial found no gain in daily functioning or quality of life. The VA/DoD guideline still rates the evidence as insufficient. Treat a service dog as one possible addition to therapy, and talk with your treatment team before you apply.

Frequently Asked Questions

What tasks can a PTSD service dog be trained to do?

ADA guidance lists calming a person with PTSD during an anxiety attack as an example of a service dog task. The dog must be individually trained to do work that relates to the person's disability. Comfort from the dog's presence alone does not count as a task under the ADA.

Do I need a certificate or vest for a PTSD service dog?

No. ADA rules say staff cannot require a special identification card or training documentation for the dog. Staff may ask only if the dog is a service animal required because of a disability and what task it is trained to perform.

Is an emotional support dog as good as a service dog for PTSD?

In the VA trial, veterans with either type of dog had similar functioning and quality of life over 18 months. Service dogs gave a 3.7-point greater reduction in PTSD symptom scores. An emotional support dog does not have a service dog's public access rights under the ADA.

Have service dogs been studied in civilians with PTSD?

The two main controlled studies enrolled veterans and military members. The University of Arizona authors note that their findings may not apply to people who do not seek out service dogs. The evidence for civilians is therefore less certain.

Will VA pay the vet bills for a PTSD service dog?

VA can cover veterinary insurance for an approved service dog, but approval is not automatic for PTSD. A VA mental health provider and care team must find that the mental health condition is the primary cause of substantial mobility limits. The dog must come from an organization accredited by Assistance Dogs International or the International Guide Dog Federation.
