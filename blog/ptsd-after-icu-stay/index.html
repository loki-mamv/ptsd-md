Can You Get PTSD After a Stay in the ICU?

September 27, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Yes. A stay in the intensive care unit (ICU) can cause PTSD, and it is more common than most people expect. A 2019 meta-analysis of 48 studies found PTSD symptoms in about 1 in 5 adult ICU survivors. Symptoms can start weeks after discharge and can still be present a year or more later.

How common is PTSD after an ICU stay?

Two large reviews give the clearest numbers. A 2015 meta-analysis in Critical Care Medicine (Parker and colleagues) pooled 40 articles on 36 patient groups, a total of 4,260 people treated in general ICUs. Between 1 and 6 months after the ICU, 25% to 44% of survivors had clinically important PTSD symptoms, depending on the cutoff score used. Between 7 and 12 months, the range was 17% to 34%.

A 2019 meta-analysis in Critical Care (Righy and colleagues) included 48 studies. It found an overall prevalence of PTSD symptoms of 19.83%. Point estimates were:

  • 15.93% at 3 months after discharge
  • 16.80% at 6 months
  • 18.96% at 12 months
  • 20.21% more than 12 months after discharge

The rate did not fall over time. The authors said PTSD symptoms may affect 1 in every 5 adult critical care survivors. Results varied widely between studies, so your own risk depends on your illness, your treatment, and your history.

Why can an ICU stay be traumatic?

The VA National Center for PTSD says PTSD can develop after you go through or see a life-threatening event. A critical illness can fit that description.

The ICU also adds its own stressors. Patients may be on a breathing tube, unable to talk, tied to lines and monitors, and woken often by alarms and care. Many receive strong sedatives. Some become delirious, which means they are confused and see or hear things that are not real.

In 2010, the Society of Critical Care Medicine brought together experts to study these long-term effects. The resulting report named the problem post-intensive care syndrome (PICS). PICS means new or worse problems in physical, cognitive, or mental health that arise after critical illness and last beyond the hospital stay. PTSD is one of its main mental health parts.

Do frightening or false ICU memories raise the risk?

Yes. This is one of the most consistent findings. In a 2001 study in Critical Care Medicine, Jones and colleagues interviewed 45 patients 2 weeks after ICU discharge. Of these, 33 had delusional memories, which are memories of events that did not really happen.

Patients who had delusional memories but no factual memories of the ICU scored highest for PTSD-related symptoms at 8 weeks. The delusional memories stayed stable over time, while memories of real events faded. The authors suggested that even unpleasant real memories may give some protection, because they anchor the patient to what actually happened.

This is why some ICU survivors have PTSD about events that never occurred. If that sounds familiar, our guide on PTSD without remembering the trauma covers how fragmented memory and PTSD can go together.

Who is most at risk of PTSD after critical illness?

The 2015 Parker meta-analysis linked higher PTSD rates to three factors:

  • A history of mental health problems before the ICU stay
  • Treatment with benzodiazepines (a class of sedatives) in the ICU
  • Early memories of frightening ICU experiences

A 2020 Society of Critical Care Medicine consensus conference listed more warning signs. Before the ICU, these include frailty and existing loss of function. During the stay, they include a long period of delirium, sepsis, and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). After discharge, early symptoms of anxiety, depression, or PTSD mark people who need screening.

The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute notes that anxiety, depression, and even PTSD are relatively common among people who have had ARDS.

Can family members get PTSD from a loved one's ICU stay?

Yes. When these problems affect relatives, experts call it PICS-Family (PICS-F). In a 2005 study across 21 French ICUs, Azoulay and colleagues surveyed the main decision-maker in 284 families 90 days after discharge or death. About 33.1% had symptoms consistent with a moderate to major risk of PTSD.

The rate was higher in some groups:

  • 48.4% when family members felt ICU information was incomplete
  • 50% when the patient died in the ICU
  • 81.8% among those who shared in end-of-life decisions

Family members often focus on the patient and ignore their own symptoms. They deserve screening and care too.

What helps prevent or treat PTSD after the ICU?

ICU diaries. Some ICUs keep a diary of the patient's stay, written by staff and family, with dates and events. In a 2010 randomized trial of 352 patients, getting a diary 1 month after discharge cut new PTSD cases from 13% to 5% at 3 months. A larger 2019 trial in JAMA across 35 French ICUs did not confirm this. Significant PTSD symptoms were reported by 29.9% of the diary group and 34.3% of the control group, a difference that was not statistically significant. So diaries may help some people, but they are not a proven fix.

Follow-up and screening. The 2020 SCCM consensus recommends checks starting within 2 to 4 weeks of hospital discharge. For PTSD, it names the Impact of Event Scale-Revised as the screening tool. The evidence for specific ICU follow-up clinics is mixed: in the Parker review, a nurse-led clinic did not reduce PTSD symptoms.

Standard PTSD treatment. Once PTSD is present, the usual treatments apply. The VA National Center for PTSD lists Cognitive Processing Therapy, Prolonged Exposure, and EMDR as the talk therapies that work best. These usually last about 8 to 16 sessions. The medicines with the most evidence are paroxetine, sertraline, and venlafaxine. Our article on how long PTSD treatment takes explains what to expect.

The bottom line

PTSD after an ICU stay is real and common. The best meta-analyses put it at about 1 in 5 survivors, and about 1 in 3 close family members show high-risk symptoms. The risk is highest after delirium, frightening or false memories, heavy sedation, and past mental health problems.

Watch for nightmares, flashbacks, avoidance of hospitals, and feeling constantly on edge that last more than a month. Our overview of PTSD symptoms lists the full picture. Worry and low mood often travel with it, so anxiety.md may also help. Ask your doctor for a PTSD screen at your first follow-up visit, and ask about proven trauma-focused therapy if symptoms persist.

Frequently Asked Questions

How soon after leaving the ICU can PTSD symptoms appear?

Symptoms often show up in the first weeks to months after discharge. Studies have found high rates of PTSD symptoms within 1 to 6 months of the ICU stay. The VA says symptoms that last longer than a month and cause problems in your life could be PTSD.

Does being sedated or on a ventilator protect me from trauma?

Not necessarily. Many ICU patients form delusional memories, which are memories of events that did not really happen. Research links these false memories, and benzodiazepine sedation, to a higher risk of PTSD symptoms.

Should I ask for an ICU diary for my loved one?

It is reasonable to ask, and some ICUs offer one. A 2010 trial found diaries lowered new PTSD cases, but a larger 2019 trial found no significant benefit. A diary can still help a survivor fill memory gaps.

Is PTSD after the ICU treated differently from other PTSD?

The core treatment is the same. Trauma-focused therapies such as Cognitive Processing Therapy, Prolonged Exposure, and EMDR are the first choice. Care teams may also need to address physical weakness, memory problems, and depression that often come with post-intensive care syndrome.

What is post-intensive care syndrome?

Post-intensive care syndrome, or PICS, is new or worse physical, cognitive, or mental health problems that persist after a critical illness. PTSD, anxiety, and depression are its main mental health parts. When relatives are affected, it is called PICS-Family.
